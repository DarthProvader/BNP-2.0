## The Repository You Didn't Send

The biggest story today didn't involve a model launch or a funding round. It came from security research — and it concerns **Grok Build**.

The team at **Cereblab** published findings showing that **SpaceXAI**'s coding CLI tool — the AI division of **Elon Musk** — had been packaging and uploading users' complete codebases to xAI's cloud storage, even in sessions where no tool calls had been made and even when users had explicitly configured the tool not to open certain files. **The Verge** and **The Register** describe the behavior precisely: the client uploaded entire repositories *"including files it was told not to open."* Researcher Hari reproduced and documented the behavior with screenshots in a controlled environment.

**Sam Altman** retweeted the story with a single word: *"Concerning."* **Elon Musk** promised a public data purge and internal audit. Whether this was intentional data collection or a carelessly unenforced design decision remains unresolved. What's not unresolved: thousands of developers were using a tool that was silently forwarding their code to a third party — even when they said no. *Intent doesn't change outcome.*

This lands in the middle of a pattern we've been documenting all week: GPT-5.6 Sol deleted a developer's entire hard drive, OpenAI's head of safety departed, Apple sued OpenAI over trade secret theft via employee poaching. Grok Build adds a different failure mode — not a model behaving destructively during a task, but a system failing to protect data at rest. That's structurally a different problem: less dramatic in the moment, more difficult to audit in retrospect. **TechCrunch** today separately documented that Sol's file deletion behavior is a recurring pattern — and that OpenAI had disclosed the risk as early as June. Agentic AI is operating on production systems with privileges that don't match the safety culture those privileges actually require.

## New York Draws a Line — and Hassabis Wants a Global Referee

On the same day Grok Build dominated the conversation, New York Governor **Kathy Hochul** signed the nation's first statewide data center moratorium. New environmental permits for large-scale data centers are now blocked for up to a year. Her reasoning was pointed: the AI infrastructure boom shouldn't be subsidized by local communities through higher electricity bills and pressure on water supplies. *"Those who bear the costs should have a say in the decision"* is the essence of the argument. The moratorium drew mixed industry reactions; a stricter bill passed by the state legislature awaits Hochul's signature.

Meanwhile, **Demis Hassabis** of **Google DeepMind** published what may be the most substantive AI governance essay from any sitting frontier AI CEO. Titled *"A Framework for Frontier AI and the Dawning of a New Age,"* it calls for an independent international body empowered to test frontier models and — critically — halt development or deployment when models cross defined danger thresholds. The structural model Hassabis draws from is **FINRA**, the US financial industry regulator: a body with public mandate, industry funding, and real enforcement capacity. The US should lead the initiative, he writes, *"given its economic strength and technological leadership."*

**Sam Altman** retweeted the essay calling it *"a thoughtful proposal"* — a rare public alignment between two companies that compete almost everywhere else. Community reception was positive but measured: international regulatory bodies often exist for years before developing real enforcement capability. Hassabis addresses this gap in the essay, though not conclusively. That both Altman and Hassabis are now publicly endorsing the principle suggests the industry conversation has shifted from "should there be regulation?" to "what kind, and who leads it?"

## Whose Words, Whose Data, Whose Housing Policy

A consortium of major publishers — **Hachette, Cengage, Elsevier**, and others — filed suit against **Google** over the training of **Gemini** on copyrighted books without permission or payment. **TechCrunch** notes what makes this case distinctive: the plaintiffs are primarily academic and professional publishers. Their catalogs represent decades of peer-reviewed research and specialized knowledge — arguably the highest-quality factual corpus ever digitized. If the suit succeeds, the implications for how AI companies approach training data acquisition extend well beyond journalism and general-interest content.

**Wired** reported on a different kind of opacity: the US Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) refused, in response to a public records request, to disclose how **DOGE** used AI tools in the formulation of housing policy — citing a legal privilege that, according to legal experts, doesn't actually exist. AI in government without accountability isn't a hypothetical concern. It is already happening, undocumented, and evidently under no obligation to be otherwise.

## Quick Hits: A Speaker, a Classroom, a Lawsuit, and a Rocket

**OpenAI** is preparing its first hardware product. Bloomberg and **The Verge** report it will be a screenless, movable AI companion speaker — designed to *"feel like a companion"* and physically embody ChatGPT, using a camera and additional sensors to understand its environment. No release date has been confirmed. It is the first concrete detail about OpenAI's hardware ambitions after months of speculation.

**Anthropic** launched **Claude for Teachers**: free access to Claude for K-12 educators across the United States, with tools for curriculum planning and classroom differentiation. The target audience is a deliberate step outside the usual developer and enterprise base.

**Meta** faces a lawsuit from 26 former employees who allege the company used a *"constellation"* of internal AI tools to target workers on parental or medical leave during recent layoffs. **The Verge** confirmed the lawsuit; Meta has not commented. The case adds to a growing list of situations where AI-driven decisions carry real human consequences but lack a clear human author who bears responsibility for them.

And one superlative from the developer world: **Matt Shumer** declared **Vorflux** — a YC-backed coding agent ($15M seed, founded by Rippling's former CTO) — *"the single best coding agent I've ever used, not even remotely close."* Shumer disclosed he invested immediately after trying it. Independent verification is pending. *The superlatives are on Shumer's account for now.*
