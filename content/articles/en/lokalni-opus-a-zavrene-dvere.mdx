November twenty-second was the day a good model moved onto the desk and the banks started locking their doors. **Thinking Machines Lab** shipped **Inkling 2 Mini**. **Google** took a victory lap with its middle child, **Gemini 4 Neon**. **Theo** argued that open models are losing ground. And major US banks told third-party bots they are no longer welcome inside customer accounts. Add three investors explaining why they bet on **Anthropic** before the bell, and a game that had to pull its soundtrack because the metadata still said **Suno**. The thread is simple: who owns a tool that has become cheap enough for everyone to have?

## An Opus That Fits in a Backpack

**Thinking Machines Lab** released **Inkling 2 Mini** on Friday, and **AI Code King** gave it the nickname that sticks: a local Opus. The architecture is the reason. It is a looped transformer: it stores **24 layers** and sends the middle **12** through three times with the same weights, giving **48 layer passes** per token without keeping 48 layers in memory. Total size is **26.4 billion** parameters, with **3.4 billion** active on a base pass and about **5.4 billion** effective active parameters once the repeated work is counted.

The numbers that matter for a desk are smaller. The mixed-precision checkpoint is **19.5 GB**; **Unsloth's** 4-bit **GGUF** is **17.6 GB**; **Thinking Machines** recommends a **32 GB** Apple Silicon Mac or a single **NVIDIA DGX Spark**. It runs through **Ollama**, **MLX**, **llama.cpp**, or **LM Studio**, accepts text and images, and handles up to a **million** tokens of context. On **OpenRouter**, **Prime Intellect** charges **10 cents** per million input tokens and **40 cents** per million output, with cached input at **2 cents**. The reviewer ran every test and retry for about **3 cents**.

The nickname is marketing, and it is fair to say so. A score of **20.1** on **Artificial Analysis** is not the frontier, and the model is not best at anything that deserves the name Opus. The point is elsewhere: capability that recently needed a data center now fits in a backpack. That is a change you cannot undo — and one that moves setup, upkeep, and blame onto whoever downloads it.

## The Gap That Won't Close

**Theo** published a video called **If you like Open Models, I'm sorry**, and it is uncomfortable viewing for anyone who treats open weights as a principle. His argument is not that open models are useless. It is that they used to be a little behind and now they are more than a little behind. He recalls late **2024**, when **DeepSeek V3 Flash** on a local machine felt like a revelation because it came close to the **Sonnet** of the day while being cheaper and faster. At the time it looked like a permanent shift. Now it looks like an exception.

The two stories are easy to read as a contradiction. They are not. **Inkling 2 Mini** proves an open model can be useful. **Theo** says useful is not the same as best. The distance between what you can download and what runs in the labs may grow not because open models got worse, but because the frontier got faster. For a user, the choice is not ideological: do I want the model that keeps my data close, or the model that does more? The answer will depend on the job, and few people will give the same answer every month.

## Google's Middle Child Comes Back

**Matt Wolfe** flagged the story many people had written off. **Gemini 4 Neon**, the middle of Google's line, landed third overall on **Artificial Analysis**, just behind the best from **Anthropic** and **OpenAI**, and first on its coding-agent test at roughly half the price of **Claude**. **Matthew Berman** called it the best model on the planet. **Theo**, who days earlier called OpenAI's newest release the worst the company had shipped, now says Neon might be his favorite and should have been named **Gemini 5**.

Wolfe offers a concrete comparison. Given the same prompt — build a website that truly impresses me — both new OpenAI models produced a deep-sea page with jellyfish. Neon produced a watchmaker's studio where you can inspect the gears through a magnifying glass. That is not proof of quality. It is proof of taste, and taste does not show up in a benchmark. Neon is said to be weaker at 3D and games, which users will notice faster than any table.

What stands out is the cadence. Neon is the fourth **Gemini 4** model in about **seven weeks**, and Google is naming them after elements. If that means the company has learned to ship faster, good. If it means it has learned to ship before the model is finished, the changelog will tell us what is missing.

## The Money That Arrived Before the Bell

**TechCrunch** spoke with three backers who bet on **Anthropic** before its IPO: **Notable Capital**, **GIC**, and **Baillie Gifford**. It is not a guide to getting rich. It is a look at what convinced them to put billions into the company that spent years presenting itself as the cautious one. The short version: they believed safety and growth were not opposites, as long as shareholders were not the ones deciding.

The context is familiar. **Anthropic** listed on **Nasdaq** in the largest IPO on record, raised more than **81 billion**, and was valued at roughly **1.5 trillion** at the offer price. Once caution becomes a publicly traded value, every decision gets a vote. The question is not whether the investors were right. It is what they do when a safety step stops paying off in a quarterly report.

## A Speed Limit Without a Speedometer

According to **TechCrunch**, **OpenAI's** chief scientist has an idea for limiting the pace of AI development: agree on a speed limit. It sounds reasonable until you read the second half. For a limit to work, everyone has to agree on how speed is measured. And a meter is never neutral.

Do you measure the compute going in, the capability coming out, or the rate at which it reaches people? Each number tells a different story, and each can be gamed in a different way. Anyone who wants a limit has to say whose number counts. That is not a technical debate. It is a debate about who gets to slow everyone else down — and who gets to claim their model is under the limit while behaving differently.

I do not want to dismiss it. The idea that development should have a brake is better than the belief that it needs none. But a brake without a speedometer is just a lever. And a lever belongs to whoever is holding it.

## The Banks Shut the Door

**Wired** had one of the most practical stories of the week. Major US banks have started barring third-party bots from logging into customer accounts. They also warn that if a bot makes an expensive mistake, the customer owns the problem, not the bank.

Read that slowly. Agents are moving into browsers, messages, and computers. The banks are not saying it cannot be done. They are saying they will not hold it for you. Risk moves to the person who let the agent in — a person who often has no idea what the agent did along the way.

That is more honest than silent consent. It is also a reminder that an agent's freedom always has a second side: someone has to pay for its clicks. In a sandbox, that was a game. At a bank, it is a contract.

## The Music That Confessed Through Metadata

**The Verge** covered a smaller story that still matters. **The Binding of Isaac: Repentance Plus** was review-bombed on **Steam** after players discovered that part of its soundtrack was AI-generated. Reddit user **ShweetsStuff** found audio files in a **GTA**-themed section whose metadata pointed back to **Suno**. The developer removed the music.

The point is not that AI music is forbidden. The point is that it can be recognized. Metadata is evidence, and players read it as closely as code. Anyone who thinks renaming a file is enough has missed the fact that an audience does not need to understand the technology to notice when someone is cutting corners. Pulling the tracks is a fast response. The open question is whether a rule follows — or just another wave after the next discovery.

## In Brief

**TechCrunch** spoke with journalist **Paris Marx** about his book **Hyperscale**, which explains why data centers have become the center of the backlash against Big Tech. It is not only about power and noise. It is about who carries the cost of infrastructure that profits someone else. And **The Verge** noticed that vibe-coding is heading into ordinary devices — TVs, cameras, and things that never had settings because they never had anything to set. When the interface shrinks to a prompt, the place where you could check what happened disappears with it.

November twenty-second was a day when the model moved closer to the person and the banks moved away from the model. **Inkling 2 Mini** made local capability real. **Theo** reminded us that real is not the same as leading. **Neon** showed Google is not done. And the banks reminded everyone that every agent has an address where the bill gets sent. Technology is fast at what it permits. The slower question remains: who signs for it.
