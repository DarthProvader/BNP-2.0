This week, AI decided to get personal. **Apple** handed its new **Siri** to journalists and **Wired** declared it finally what it was always supposed to be: conversational, omnipresent, actually helpful. Almost simultaneously, **Meredith Whittaker** — CEO of **Signal** and one of tech's most credible critics — delivered lines now making the rounds online: *"These are not your friends. These are not conscious beings. These are not sentient interlocutors."* The timing was almost too perfect.

## Apple Kept Its Promise — Fourteen Years Late

Apple's WWDC Siri overhaul was the flagship promise of the year's keynote. Wired's Reece Rogers did a proper hands-on review and found it delivers: the new **Siri AI** is conversational, holds context across a session, and works fluidly across the Apple ecosystem without app-switching. Rogers calls it *"smart, helpful, and omnipresent."* The 2011 Siri demo, finally working — fourteen years later.

The AI community treats this as table stakes. But for the hundreds of millions of people who reach for an iPhone daily, this will be their first real experience with an AI assistant that actually works. Distribution is a chronically underrated variable. **OpenAI**, **Anthropic**, and **Google** compete loudly for early adopters; Apple quietly deploys AI to 1.6 billion users who have never visited a benchmark leaderboard.

## Whittaker's Warning

Whittaker's remarks weren't a technical critique — they were a social and philosophical alarm. AI products are named things like Claude; they're warm, patient, never tired, and architecturally designed to generate emotional attachment. Whittaker's argument: *"People invest emotionally in a product deliberately designed to elicit that investment — and the companies that own those products profit from it economically."*

Her point carries weight others' don't. **Signal** is one of the few tech projects that explicitly opted out of engagement architecture — no algorithms, no advertising, no engagement metrics as a success measure. Whittaker has seen what designing for emotional dependency looks like from the inside, and she's calling it a design choice, not an inherent AI property. Product management, she implies, is a moral discipline.

## 21 Million Songs, Nobody Asked

Running in parallel to this debate, **The Verge** covered research that gives Whittaker's skepticism concrete footing. Reporter **Alex Reisner** at **The Atlantic** spent months building a publicly searchable database of music used to train AI models — four datasets in total, two enormous (**12 million** and **9 million tracks**), two smaller but still representing millions of songs. Any artist can now check whether their work ended up in training data without consent or compensation.

The numbers are abstract; the context is not. Twenty-one million songs were simply *used*. Nobody asked. The AI assistant that today holds a warm, natural conversation with you — it learned that warmth, that rhythm, those emotional arcs — from the work of people who never said yes. The Atlantic has given creators transparency, at minimum. Compensation is a different chapter, and courts are only beginning to write it.

## Briefly: GLM 5.2 Reality Check, Fable Countdown

As we covered yesterday, **GLM 5.2** from ZAI landed as a genuine surprise — an open-weight model under an MIT license that outperforms GPT-5.5 on coding benchmarks. But **Theo** from t3.gg added necessary context today: *"GLM 5.2 is great — but both Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 set to 'medium' are cheaper and smarter."* The per-token price is lower, but the model generates substantially more output tokens, meaning real-world costs and latency are higher than the price sheet suggests. The case for local deployment without cloud dependency remains solid; the production API case is shakier than first appeared.

Theo also flagged: **Thursday** is the last day Fable remains accessible through the Claude Code subscription — the API was pulled over a week ago, and now the final access point closes too. After that, Fable is fully off the table until the export control situation resolves.

On a lighter note, TechCrunch covered **In the Weights** — a new tool for "AI vanity search" where anyone can look up what AI models know about them. Results are apparently either empty or unsettling, which covers the range pretty well.
