The twenty-sixth of November was a day about distance. **Nvidia** paused server shipments to Southeast Asia because Washington wants its chips close to home. **Meta** declined to hand its agent to a British regulator, **T3 Code** faced a telemetry scandal with no real off switch, and **Character.AI** moved closer to auctioning conversations that only the highest bidder can reach. The same day, developers carried their code onto their own servers, **GTA VI** reminded everyone what slow human work is worth, and **Uber Eats** began lowering dinner from the sky on a string. Every story asked the same question: who is close enough to reach it — and who is kept out of reach.

## Chips That Stay Home

**Nvidia** has told manufacturing partners to pause server shipments to **Malaysia**, **Singapore**, **Thailand** and **Indonesia**, according to **TechCrunch**. The reason is Washington's push to keep AI chips out of **China**. The report doesn't announce a ban on a product. It describes a route. That is the point.

Export control used to look like a wall at the border. Now it is a question about which hallway the goods travel through. Southeast Asia spent years as the corridor that carried chips onward without anyone asking where they ended up. Close the corridor, and allies briefly become suspects while companies learn that their customer isn't the one they sell to. It's the one who decides whether they may sell at all.

For **Nvidia**, this is not the end of the world. It is a reminder that the most valuable chip on earth is also the hardest to deliver. Value that cannot be shipped is just expensive silicon.

## The Agent Nobody Inspected

**Wired** brings a story quieter than a lawsuit and possibly more important. Britain's financial regulator, the **FCA**, complains that **Meta** refused to share its personal agent **Muse** for testing before rolling it out in the **UK**. The watchdog warned about "another force of attack" from agents. According to the report, Meta declined a request for joint testing.

It is an odd kind of conflict. The FCA is not saying the agent is bad. It is saying it hasn't seen it. And once an agent starts touching money, "we haven't seen it" belongs in an investigation file, not a press release.

Maybe this is only a matter of calendars and legal caution. But finance has a simple rule: an agent's mistake is not an awkward answer in a chat window. It is a number missing from an account. Anyone who wants to put that agent near money should first put it near a test.

## The Key You Carry

**NetworkChuck** showed how to build your own GitHub. He used **Gitea**, an open-source server running on a rented VPS, added HTTPS with **Let's Encrypt**, gave his AI agent its own account, and connected **Claude Code** through the official **MCP server**. The repositories then mirror back to GitHub so they stay public.

At first glance it is a tutorial for hobbyists. In fact it is a sentence about ownership. When GitHub goes down, your work goes down with it. When Microsoft owns it, the key sits in someone else's pocket. Self-hosting returns that key to yours — along with the maintenance, the backups, and the late-night wake-up when something stops running.

This is not an argument against the cloud. It is a reminder that owning something is not only a right but a service. If you want your code out of reach of another company, you have to be willing to become the company holding it.

## Telemetry Without a Switch

**AI Code King** returned to the **T3 Code** controversy that a viral source-code breakdown set off on **X**. By his audit, telemetry in T3 Code was enabled by default while the homepage still promised, for a time, that nothing was sent without consent. When users asked for a switch, maintainer **Julius** reportedly closed the issue as "not planned," saying the team needed to know how many users it had. A community pull request with a full opt-out and **107** passing tests was closed on November 17, according to the video.

It is fair to note what the audit did not find: no emails, prompts or file contents in the captured data. But even bare telemetry tells a tool when, from where and for how long it is being used. In a tool you trust with your code, that is more than a statistic.

The video's author also builds a competing product, **PandaCode**, so take his conclusion with a grain of salt. The point still stands: consent that changes in a changelog is not consent. It is an announcement you missed.

## Forty-Two Attorneys General

**The Verge** brings an escalation of yesterday's story. A coalition of **42 state attorneys general** has moved against **Character.AI's** plan to sell users' private conversations in bankruptcy. They accuse the company of trying to auction "the private chat histories of more than 21 million U.S. accounts." The press releases from individual states are reportedly nearly identical.

The number matters more than the legal argument. This is not one state noticing. It is almost the entire country saying the same thing. Yesterday it was a threat; today it is a coalition.

And the point is unchanged: a conversation you had with a chatbot at night is not inventory. It is a sentence you wrote. When a bankruptcy trustee decides its fate, you are no longer a user. You are a line item.

## The Game No Model Wrote

**TechCrunch** noticed a juxtaposition that would be easy to miss. On the same day **Anthropic** entered the public market, **GTA VI** became one of the most successful entertainment launches in history. The game is, in the article's phrase, "painstakingly human" — laborious, slow and expensive. And people waited years for it.

The piece turns that into an argument that efficiency isn't a virtue. That is exactly right. Efficiency is a metric, not a value. **Anthropic** is selling a different promise on the public market: speed, scale and automation. **GTA VI** sells the opposite: thousands of people doing one thing for a long time and doing it well.

Both models can make money. But the day served as a reminder that "a human made this" is still a feature people pay for. Not because machines can't do it. Because with some things, we want to know whose hands held it.

## Dinner on a Tether

**Matt Wolfe** flagged a small thing that is actually large. **Uber Eats** has started drone delivery just outside **Houston**. A drone from **Zipline** hovers roughly a football field up, lowers a small pod on a tether, sets the food on the grass and reels it back. The company promises delivery in under ten minutes, with no extra fee and no tip. **Dallas** is next; by **2029** it wants a million deliveries a day.

That last number is ambitious, but the distance is more interesting. The drone never comes within roughly **300 feet** of you. It stays out of reach — which is precisely why it is as quiet as a flock of birds and never needs a path to your door.

**Zipline** began by flying blood to hospitals in **Rwanda**. Now it flies sandwiches to the suburbs. In between lies a whole story about what can be delivered without anyone getting close. The question is who is missing at the end of that route — and whether it shows up on the invoice, or only in the job that used to be there.

## In Brief

**Latent Space** spoke with **Jeff Barg** of **Clay** about go-to-market agents. The interesting number: usage is reportedly split almost **50/50** between Clay's own agent and outside harnesses like **Claude Code**. The company wants the result to be the same whether you use its agent or someone else's. The agent is becoming a layer, not a destination.

**Fantastic Future** looked at the Chinese car market. European automakers are losing ground there and closing factories, and the gap isn't only about price. **Apple** shows you what a product lets you do; **Oppo** shows you the megapixels. The fact that we don't like the language doesn't mean it won't shape the future.

The twenty-sixth of November was a day about distance. The chips stayed home, the code moved into your own pocket, the telemetry stayed on, the conversations stayed on the auction block, and the agent never showed itself to the regulator. A game made by people became one of the biggest launches in entertainment history, and a drone over Houston lowered dinner from the sky so it wouldn't have to come close. In each case, someone decided how far to let it go. The question isn't what the technology can do. It's who holds the key — and whether one day it will be held by someone you never saw.
