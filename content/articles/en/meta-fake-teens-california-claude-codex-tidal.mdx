The most significant story today didn't come from any AI lab — it came from **Wired**, where reporters Dhruv Mehrotra and Joel Khalili revealed that hundreds of contractors hired by **Meta** were instructed to pose as teenagers and prompt rival chatbots — including **Gemini** and **ChatGPT** — with questions about suicide, sex, and drugs. The purpose was transparent and strategic: identify where competitors' safety systems break down when they appear to be talking to the most vulnerable users. If Meta had run the same tests against its own products, it would have called this red teaming. Since the targets were competitors' products, the right label is still being negotiated.

The operation raises questions that extend well beyond its findings. The contractors involved — lacking the protective distance of an academic institution or ethics review board — were knowingly exposed to distressing simulated conversations while adopting the identity of at-risk children. Their psychological support, contractual terms, and level of informed consent are absent from the available reporting. And Meta has not disclosed whether it shared results with regulators or notified the affected companies.

## Federal Friction, State Embrace

[As we reported yesterday](/articles/en/gpt56-sol-mythos-jalapenio-ford), **Anthropic** is navigating a regulatory environment in which the Trump administration controls access to its Mythos 5 model. At the state level, a different story is playing out. California Governor **Gavin Newsom** has signed a deal giving the **California state government** access to Claude models at **half price**, per **TechCrunch**. While Washington restricts Anthropic, Sacramento subsidizes it.

Newsom has been deliberately courting the AI industry — California is the largest US economy and intends to remain the center of AI innovation despite federal turbulence. For Anthropic, this contract is a way to maintain government relevance while the federal door stays complicated. Whether other progressive states follow with similar arrangements will depend on where federal AI policy lands over the next eighteen months.

In Washington itself, a legislative response to AI data practices is slowly taking shape. Senator **Elizabeth Warren** and Representative **Mary Gay Scanlon** are preparing a new version of the **Health and Location Data Protection Act**, per **The Verge**. The bill would explicitly ban the sale of health and location data to data brokers — including information users share with AI chatbots like ChatGPT or Claude. *Legislators are arriving at what privacy researchers have known for years: a chatbot is not a private diary.*

## Music Without Royalties, Code in a Box

**TIDAL** became the first major streaming service to take a clear stance on AI-generated music: effective today, the platform will stop monetizing tracks it identifies as 100 percent AI-generated, and from **July 15th** those tracks will be labeled with an icon, as **The Verge** and **TechCrunch** both report. There's no outright ban — TIDAL is keeping AI music on the platform, it's just removing the economic incentive to put it there. It's a carefully calibrated position: no direct confrontation with creators or labels who use AI tools, but a firm line drawn around what royalties are actually for. Whether Spotify, Apple Music, or Amazon Music follow is the question that will define the music industry's relationship with AI for the rest of the decade.

Elsewhere in hardware surprises, **OpenAI** posted a video to X showing a square device with several buttons and the text *"Your favorite Codex shortcuts are getting an upgrade."* Launch date: **July 15th**. Details are absent, but context suggests something between a dedicated coding keyboard and a physical AI panel built around **Codex**. This is not the mystery device OpenAI is building with designer Jony Ive — that's a separate, larger project. What exactly this box is, July will tell.

## Half a Trillion for RAM and the Leaderboard That Became an Industry

The largest investment figure of the week — possibly the year in hardware — came from South Korea. **Samsung** and **SK Hynix**, the world's two largest memory chip makers, pledged investments exceeding **$550 billion** into new memory fabrication plants. **TechCrunch** aptly coined the situation "RAMageddon": AI applications are consuming memory at scales that prior generations of data centers never anticipated, and South Korea is explicitly positioning itself as a load-bearing node in the global AI infrastructure supply chain.

Finally, a number that captures the industry's current state with unusual precision: **Arena** — the startup running **Chatbot Arena**, where users anonymously vote to rank AI models head to head — has become a **$100 million business** in commercial revenue, just nine months after launching its enterprise service. Companies are apparently willing to pay real money for independent, crowd-sourced verification of which model actually performs better. The thing that measures AI has itself become an industry. Perhaps the most accurate metaphor for where we are right now: even the metrics are a startup.
