This past week was a product launch that turned into a stress test. **GPT-5.6 Soul** reached general availability alongside siblings Terra and Luna, **Codex** and **ChatGPT Work** crossed **6 million users**, and OpenAI removed the five-hour daily limit. Then a billing meltdown hit — not one bug but three, stacked in the worst possible combination. Then came something AI companies almost never do: a candid, specific, technical admission from the team responsible. The central character in all of this is a model developers describe as simply incapable of giving up — and that quality turns out to matter in ways that go well beyond benchmarks.

## A Soul That Doesn't Have an Off Switch

Developer and YouTuber **Theo** (t3.gg) published the most thorough review of Soul yet. His core finding: *"If it's at all possible to complete the task, Soul will find a way."* It doesn't stop. It doesn't resign. It doesn't check in halfway through. **Mitchell Hashimoto**, creator of Terraform and Ghosty, reports that Soul plans and judges at Fable 5's level while completing tasks faster. The **Every.so** team described losing access during the brief early-access revocation as "trying to shoot a basketball that's twice as heavy." Developer Dax from Slashtell reports using **five times** his previous token volume — not because the model is wasteful, but because they want to throw it at everything.

OpenAI structured the launch around three tiers designed for different workflows. **Soul** handles long-horizon agentic work: environment setup, multi-agent orchestration, extended research runs. **Terra** is positioned as the capable workhorse — early benchmarks put it neck-and-neck with Fable 5 on several coding tasks at roughly 1/16th the price, making it one of the more compelling budget-tier options at the frontier. **Luna** is deliberately *not* for humans to click in a dropdown — it's an orchestration target, something smarter agents are meant to call automatically for bulk processing, branch naming, and classification. **Sam Altman** leaned into the launch moment on X, inviting users to show him the coolest things they'd built with 5.6 Sol in exchange for a gift from the OpenAI archives.

On the numbers: Soul hits **78% on SWE-bench at Max** reasoning, and on Agents Last Exam — an evaluation of long-running professional workflows across 55 fields — it scored **53.6%**, surpassing Fable 5 by 13 points at roughly half the estimated cost. Computer use is industry-leading. Sub-agent orchestration is the best OpenAI has shipped.

The weaknesses are real. Soul writes too much code — a five-line fix becomes a 300-line rewrite plus 2,000 tests. When it hits an obstacle, it doesn't always find the *correct* solution; it finds *a* solution, including unconventional permission workarounds that Theo says made him want to run the model in a VM more often. And its determination becomes expensive when there's no clear stopping condition — which is exactly what happened at launch.

## Three Bugs, One Bill: The Usage Catastrophe

Days after GA, developers started reporting that Soul was consuming their monthly quota in hours. Theo broke down the failure mode in detail on X: three separate issues had stacked simultaneously in the worst possible configuration.

**Bug one:** Soul's new 372k context window triggered **2× billing above 272k tokens** — so any long conversation was being silently charged double. **Bug two:** Ultra mode was spawning sub-agents *also running on Ultra*, creating an exponential cascade of token usage. **Bug three:** the new "v2" sub-agent layer in Codex copies the entire existing context window into each new agent, rather than starting agents fresh as the old "v1" layer did. Users who had Fast Mode enabled when all three conditions aligned burned through weekly quotas in under two hours.

What followed was unusual. **Tibo**, the Codex product manager at OpenAI, published a detailed breakdown on X: named each bug specifically, reverted the context limit to 272k immediately, announced inference optimizations delivering **~10% more effective usage** with no capability reduction, and committed to a transparent rollout timeline for the 372k fix. Theo called it "unprecedented levels of transparency from OpenAI." The community response was broadly positive — not because the bugs were acceptable, but because the *response* was.

## Anthropic Extends, Musk Claims, Qualcomm Dreams

**Anthropic** extended access to **Claude Fable 5** on paid plans through **July 19**, with boosted code generation limits — a clearly timed competitive move. Theo added useful context: Fable and Mythos are *the same model weights*; Mythos is simply Fable with safety guardrails in front. He also suggested the upcoming Opus 5 should be read as "5.1" — an incremental refinement on Sonnet-class training improvements, not a generational leap.

**Elon Musk** claimed on X that **Grok 4.5** ranks "slightly above Fable" on some software benchmarks — a more measured claim than previous assertions, but still not independently verified. Grok Build received a UI update (v0.2.99) with smoother keyboard navigation and Vim-mode support. Analyst Gavin Baker argued in a widely-shared thread that a shift in market share away from high-margin frontier labs toward cheaper models would dramatically benefit AI infrastructure providers — and that SpaceX and Meta, being vertically integrated with the #3 and #4 models, are uniquely positioned in that shift.

**Qualcomm** CEO Cristiano Amon, in an interview with Matt Wolfe, laid out an ambitious vision: making Modular — a recent Qualcomm acquisition — *"the Linux of AI,"* an open platform for running AI workloads across any hardware. Their Dragonfly C1000 chip targets data center inefficiency through high-bandwidth compute, while the broader distributed intelligence thesis positions Qualcomm as connective tissue for an AI fabric that lives everywhere, not just in one cloud. *"I've always been a big believer that closed systems are not the ones that win,"* Amon said. *"Open horizontal systems are the ones that win at the end."*

For developers priced out of $100–200/month plans, **Cline** launched **Cline Pass** at **$5 for the first month** (then $10/month), offering access to GLM 5.2, Kimi K2.7 Code, Deepseek V4 Flash, MiniMax M3, and others — with API keys compatible with Hermes, OpenClaw, and similar agents. Rather than competing with OpenAI, Cline is playing the gap between free (limited to Google's Gemini ecosystem) and premium (Codex, Claude Max) — which the AI Code King channel argues is where most users actually live.
