November twentieth was a day that demanded evidence. A mathematician working with **Anthropic** said he had proved the **Riemann hypothesis** with **Claude**, and on the same day a human published a proof of his own. **Google** opened **Project Genie**, a generated world you can walk around. **Signal** blocked screen captures so AI agents could not read your messages. A researcher released **two million** canaries into the AI supply chain and watched where they escaped. And **METR** updated the graph everyone quotes and few read correctly. One question ties it together: who looks at this from the outside — and what do they see?

## A Hundred and Sixty-Seven Years, Two Proofs

**The Verge** reported on Friday what sounds like the end of an era in mathematics: **Levent Alpöge**, a mathematician working at **Anthropic**, claims that with **Claude** he proved the **Riemann hypothesis**. The problem is **167 years** old, has resisted the world's best mathematicians for decades, and is one of the **seven Millennium Prize Problems** for which the **Clay Institute** pays **one million dollars**.

But the same day, **TechCrunch** pointed to a second proof. A human had published a solution too, and mathematicians were reportedly stunned by both. That matters more for this newsletter than the result itself. Two proofs on one day are not just a sensational coincidence. They are two fronts meeting at the same crossroads — and the question of who gets there first stops being a question about compute speed.

The Riemann hypothesis is not a puzzle that can be solved faster. It is a claim about how the primes are distributed, and a large part of modern mathematics rests on it. Proving it means convincing people who will spend years looking for a mistake. An announcement is a beginning, not an end. That is what makes today strange: a machine and a human claim the same thing, and both are waiting for the same person — someone who reads it and says whether it holds.

## A World You Can Step Into

On the same day **Google** opened something else: **Project Genie** in **Google Labs**, built on **Genie 4**. According to **DeepMind**, this is not video but a generative simulation — text turns into a **3D world** you can enter, look around in, and move through. **AI Advantage** tried it on the **Ultra** tier and captured the contradiction perfectly: it looks like a very bad game, but it is not a game. It is a world being generated as you go.

It is not a product yet. It is an experiment, available only in some countries and only on the most expensive plan, and in the demo it falls apart if you move too fast. That is exactly why it matters. **Genie 4** does not show how well Google generates images. It shows how close we are to turning text into a space where you can get lost — and where an agent can be trained without ever stepping into the real internet.

**Wired** recently reported that Google is building a mirror-dimension internet to test browsing agents. Genie is the same idea without servers: if you do not want an agent to break the real world, build it a practice one.

## The Screen You Must Not See

**Signal**, according to **The Verge**, has restricted screenshots on both **Android** and **iOS**. The reason is not a jealous partner. It is the **agentic AI** built into operating systems, which reads what is on the display and, in the words of Signal's president, undermines the app's ability to independently guarantee privacy.

This is the first public surrender of one layer of software to another. Signal spent years promising that only the sender and the recipient can see a message. Then along comes a system with permission to watch the screen, and that sentence can no longer be said without an asterisk. Banning screenshots is not a solution. It is a sign that the privacy fight has moved from encryption to permissions.

## Two Million Canaries

**Ars Technica** published a study that measures trust in the AI supply chain in the most uncomfortable way possible. A researcher placed **two million** unique, traceable secrets — **canaries** — into prompts and watched where they went. According to **Dan Goodin**, the result shows that data is not private just because it passed through a private tool. It travels between models, vendors, and logs, and shows up in places nobody expected.

The canary in the coal mine is an old trick: when it dies, you know there is gas. Here there were two million of them, and the question is not whether some die. It is how many survive a journey nobody promised them. For a company sending customer data into a model through three vendors, that is a more expensive headline than any benchmark. It does not say the model is bad. It says the chain around it does not have a single door.

## The Graph Everyone Misreads

**MIT Technology Review** brought a new version of the **time horizon plot** from the nonprofit **METR** — a chart cited far more often than it is understood. The new report, by **Grace Huckins**, confirms that some capabilities keep improving exponentially. It also shows that models are nowhere near as good at tasks requiring **judgment** as they are at **coding**.

That distinction matters because it is becoming a political sentence. Anyone who wants to say AI will soon replace whole professions cites the curve. Anyone who wants to say it is not happening cites the same curve differently. The graph says neither. It says a machine can handle more steps in a row, and that not every step is equally hard. The gap between what a model can calculate and what it must decide does not shrink as performance grows. It simply moves somewhere harder to measure.

## Who Walks Off With the Skills

**OpenAI** published a piece called **Combatting adversarial distillation**, describing a layered defense against anyone trying to extract a model's capabilities. It combines training, safeguards, detection, and enforcement. It sounds technical, but the point is simple: a model that answers is also a manual for copying it. Anyone with enough outputs does not need the weights.

It is the same logic as the canaries, seen from the other side. There, the question was where data escapes. Here, it is who walks away with the skills. In both cases the boundary is not inside the model but in who has access and who is accountable when something gets out. And in both cases the defense is always one step behind, because the output that is supposed to be useful is also the output that can be copied.

## A Tax on Where the Servers Stand

**The Verge** carried a sentence that ties energy, politics, and AI into one: **Donald Trump** promised a "tremendous tax" on companies that build data centers outside the **United States**. "We're not going to allow our companies to build data centers outside of our country," he said. At the same time, states and cities across the country are restricting new data center applications.

It is a hundred-and-eighty-degree turn. For years the story was that compute would go wherever electricity and cooling were cheap. Now the location of the building is a political question, and a data center is something you cannot simply move. The model can live in the cloud. The concrete holding it up cannot.

## In Brief

**Tencent**, according to **AI Code King**, released **Hy4**, the first model in the **Hy** line that can see — it reads charts, screenshots, and documents and can operate a computer and a terminal. The company ranks it among leading open models, but admits it still trails the best closed ones, and the weights were not out at launch. It runs free inside **WorkBuddy** and **CodeBuddy**; in one test it caught a planted error in a fake sales dashboard, though it took twelve minutes.

**NetworkChuck** sat down with **Matt Pocock** to show how software is actually built with an agent. The point is not the prompt. AI, Pocock argues, is excellent at **tactical** programming and weak at **strategic** programming — and strategy is the human's job. His loop is boring and effective: align on the task, write a spec, split it into tickets, run sub-agents in separate worktrees, review the code, hold a retro. He keeps **Claude Opus 5.5** on medium effort, because more is not always more.

**Theo** spent a day testing and called **Gemini 4 Neon** possibly his favorite model and the first real competitor to **Opus 5.5** — smaller, cheaper, and subsidized. **Daniel Ek** backed **Europa Fusion**, a fusion startup founded by an AI researcher. **Henrijs Dandzbergs**, sixteen, dropped out of school, went through **Y Combinator**, and is building a missile startup called **Polemos** aimed at "cost per kill." And **Matt Wolfe** noted that **ChatGPT** wants to hold your card in its wallet, **Gemini Notebook** will build a website from one sentence, and **HeyGen** has shipped its sixth avatar generation.

November twentieth was a day that demanded evidence. One proof came from a mathematician and a machine, another from a human, a third from a generated world you can step into. Technology is fast at showing what it can do. The slower question remains: who stands outside and says whether it is anything more than a pretty screen.
