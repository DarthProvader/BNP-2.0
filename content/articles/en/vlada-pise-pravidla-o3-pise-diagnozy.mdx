Today's AI landscape offers one of the starkest contrasts in recent memory: while US regulators still can't explain why one model is banned, another just helped physicians diagnose rare pediatric diseases — and a Chinese open-source model quietly posted benchmarks that would have looked impossible six months ago.

## Anthropic's Regulatory Limbo, and Nobody Knows Why

**Wired** published one of the most important AI policy analyses of the year this week. The headline says it plainly: "The White House Is Making Up Its Rules for AI in Real Time." Reporter Maxwell Zeff documents that **Anthropic** still cannot distribute **Claude Mythos** or **Fable 5** — roughly three weeks after the Trump administration triggered a block under export controls. The most troubling detail? Even government officials cannot specify exactly what the company did wrong.

**The Verge**'s Decoder podcast dedicated a full episode to "Who decides when AI is too dangerous?" — senior reporter Hayden Field describes the situation to Nilay Patel as a legal fog without precedent: the sanction arrived, the rationale never did. *Rules are being written retroactively, if at all.*

The developer community is working around it. Theo of t3.gg, describing his new agent loop experiments, noted simply: "Wanted to do this with Fable. Fable was taken." He burned expensive **Opus** tokens instead — more on that below.

## o3 in the Clinic: 18 Diagnoses Doctors Couldn't Find

The most important story of the day came from **NEJM AI** — one of medicine's most prestigious publications. Researchers from **Boston Children's Hospital** and **Harvard** published a study showing that **o3 Deep Research** helped clinicians revisit previously unsolved rare pediatric disease cases, surfacing **18 new diagnoses** for families who had waited years.

Sam Altman framed it precisely: "One of the ways scaling test-time compute can benefit people most: have reasoning models think really hard about rare undiagnosed diseases." This isn't PR — it's peer-reviewed medicine. **OpenAI** also announced improvements to ChatGPT's health responses via **GPT-5.5 Instant**, developed with physician input.

Rare diseases are exactly the domain where LLMs hold a structural advantage over any individual specialist: the diagnostic challenge requires synthesizing an enormous body of literature across wildly diverse symptom profiles. A physician's diagnostic power is bounded by the cases they've personally encountered. o3 brings the full literature to every case.

## GLM-5.2 Is Knocking on the Frontier Door

China's **ZhipuAI** quietly released **GLM-5.2** — and the benchmarks make this look like considerably more than a point update. On **Terminal Bench 2.1**, the model scores **81**, trailing **Opus 4.8** (85) and **GPT-5.5** (84) but comfortably ahead of Gemini 3.1 Pro (74). On **FrontierSUE** — testing extended technical projects lasting hours or days — GLM-5.2 scores **74** versus 75 for Opus and 72 for GPT. One point from the best closed model in the world. With an **MIT license** and open weights.

ZhipuAI also launched **Z Code**, a Codex-style coding agent built specifically for GLM models, with a free tier offering **5 million tokens per day**. AI Code King reviewed the launch in depth and summarized it well: "Having this level of coding ability with open weights and an MIT license is amazing." The honest caveat: on the most demanding benchmark, **SWE-bench Ultra**, Opus still leads by a large margin (26 vs. 13 for GLM). GLM-5.2 is a frontier challenger, not a clean sweep. But placing an open-weight model closer to GPT-5.5 than Gemini 3.1 Pro is a landmark moment for the open-source AI ecosystem.

## Industry in Motion

**OpenAI** is loading up before its IPO. The company brought on **Noam Shazeer** — co-author of "Attention Is All You Need," the paper that created the transformer architecture — arriving from **Google DeepMind**, where he landed after Google acquired his startup **Character.AI**. Also joining: **Dean Ball**, a former Trump administration AI policy official, presumably relevant given the current regulatory turbulence. TechCrunch calls it bringing in "big guns in the lead-up to its IPO."

**Adobe** launched a public beta of AI assistants across its entire Creative Cloud suite — **Photoshop**, **Premiere**, **Illustrator**, **InDesign**, and **Frame.io** all receive context-aware AI chatbots. The Firefly AI studio was redesigned with persistent memory across projects. For creative professionals, this is the most significant workflow shift since Content-Aware Fill.

Three **Amazon engineers** are under internal investigation after testifying at Seattle City Council hearings about limits on data center expansion. They've filed a complaint with Seattle's civil rights office, alleging retaliation for protected political speech. Both Wired and The Verge covered the case — it may be the first documented potential employment penalty for political speech in an AI infrastructure context.

**FERC** ordered US grid operators to give data centers a priority fast lane for grid interconnections. TechCrunch correctly notes: FERC solved the queue problem, not the electricity shortage itself.

And **Theo** of t3.gg, by his own admission, burned over **$20,000 in inference in 48 hours** by building agent loops that run autonomously — filing pull requests, spinning up sub-agents to review code, addressing comments, merging, all while he slept. His video "I guess we're writing loops now?" is the most honest account yet of where developer workflows are heading: from "I ask a model a question" to "a model prompts a model that prompts a model, and I went to bed."
