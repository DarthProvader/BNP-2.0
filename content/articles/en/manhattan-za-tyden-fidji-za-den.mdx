As we covered yesterday, GPT-5.6 Sol had already begun a limited government-approved preview — but Thursday brought the full public launch, a wave of product announcements, and the White House's greenlight. The headline product alongside the model was **ChatGPT Work**: an agent powered by Codex and GPT-5.6 that can operate across apps and files, stay on a project for hours without interruption, and turn a goal into finished work. Sam Altman called it "a really big deal" while making clear that Codex isn't going anywhere — it's the engine under the hood of the new product, not a relic being replaced.

The demo that made the rounds came from Matt Shumer: **GPT-5.6-Sol** autonomously built a voxel-based Manhattan over nearly a week, with zero human intervention, producing a result that looks like a precision spatial map of a city of millions. Developer Josh Bickett noted the runtime aligns exactly with METR's time-horizon graph for agentic models. This isn't marketing theater — it's quiet empirical confirmation that the leap from "AI answers your question" to "AI works your project for a week" has quietly crossed a threshold.

Less celebrated was the simultaneous death of **ChatGPT Atlas**, OpenAI's AI-powered browser, shuttered in under a year. The Verge wrote the obituary with admirable brevity: *"The ChatGPT browser is already dead."* The pattern is familiar: launch, identify what works, discard the rest — while burying the announcement of the new product in the press release that also contains the old product's funeral. GPT-5.6's pricing also generated confusion: cloud architect QuinnyPig pointed out that nowhere in OpenAI's documentation is it clear when pricing jumps by 50%, or when the model stops being included in standard subscriptions.

## Everyone Wants a Piece

**Meta** launched **Muse Spark 1.1**, a new agentic and coding model available through the new Meta Model API. Scale AI CEO Alexandr Wang called it "industry-competitive," claiming it rivals GPT-5.5 and Opus 4.8 across multiple agentic benchmarks. It's the first time Meta has offered premium paid API access to a model in the OpenAI mold — a departure from its previous "open-source only" stance for frontier capabilities.

**xAI**'s Grok 4.5 launched yesterday, but today brought performance data. Snorkel's GDPval+ benchmark — measuring real professional workplace tasks — showed Grok 4.5 at 29% mean pass rate versus 22% for GPT 5.5 and 21% for Claude Opus 4.8. Particularly strong results came in legal work, healthcare, and QA analysis. Mathematician Paata Ivanisvili praised Grok 4.5 for independently constructing an explicit counterexample to hypercontractivity for the Poisson semigroup on the 4-sphere — a problem previously resolved only for dimensions ≤3. Musk's comment: *"Grok only gets better from here."*

**Google** quietly pushed **AlphaEvolve**, its AI code-optimization agent, into general availability.

Developer Theo (t3.gg) delivered the sharpest take of the day: *"Codex just rebranded to ChatGPT. Claude is spamming me with Excel plugins. xAI is bragging about how good Grok is at PowerPoint. I knew the 'AI labs focus 100% of their effort on software devs' thing wouldn't last, but it's sad having it end all at once."*

## The Human Cost and the Legal Heat

**Fidji Simo**, OpenAI's CEO of AGI Deployment and the company's de facto number two, announced she is stepping back from full-time work to a part-time advisory role following extended medical leave. She described it on X with disarming honesty — doctors and friends had told her to slow down for years, and she hadn't. Now she has no choice. Altman's response was visibly mournful. TechCrunch framed the situation accurately: the departure arrives as OpenAI eyes an IPO and races Anthropic for enterprise dominance — a leadership vacuum at number two, at this particular moment, is badly timed.

On the legal front, the **New York Times** escalated its copyright lawsuit, claiming OpenAI concealed tools and datasets that could have identified copyrighted journalism in ChatGPT outputs. Sanctions motions have been filed — a more serious step than a simple allegation.

## Anthropic's Paradox: Better Insight, Higher Price

**Wired** reported the subscription news that will matter to most users: Claude subscribers will soon need to pay usage-based fees on top of their base plan to access **Claude Fable 5**. Wired's framing was direct: *"the golden era of AI subscriptions is ending."* Frontier models cost more to run than flat fees can sustainably cover, and Anthropic is no longer pretending otherwise.

Anthropic also launched **Reflect**, a monthly usage dashboard that shows Claude users an analysis of their own habits — instantly nicknamed "Claude Wrapped." TechCrunch noted the feature's dual function: it's a genuine product, and it's also a masterclass in retention engineering. The more vividly you show someone how much they depend on your product, the harder it is to leave.

The story getting the least attention may be the most interesting: MIT Technology Review reports that Anthropic has developed a technique called the **Jacobian lens** — giving researchers "the clearest glimpse yet" at what actually happens inside a language model as it processes a question. What they found ranges, according to the report, from *"the mundane to the unnerving."* No further detail was provided. In the context of AI interpretability, "unnerving" is not a word researchers tend to use lightly.

Finally, **Andrew Ng** amplified an essay warning about a de facto AI licensing regime taking shape outside formal congressional frameworks — built through "voluntary agreements" with national security officials that could effectively ban open-source AI without anyone having to publicly own that decision. **Ollama**, the open-source tool that lets developers run models locally, simultaneously announced a $65 million raise and nearly 9 million users. The contrast between those two data points says something worth sitting with.
