## Cyberwar Has a New Combatant

Monday brought a milestone worth recording: **Google's Threat Intelligence Group** (GTIG) announced that it detected and neutralized, for the first time in history, a zero-day exploit *developed with AI assistance*. The threat actors — described by Google as "prominent cyber crime" operators — had planned a mass exploitation event that would have allowed them to bypass two-factor authentication on an unspecified platform. The Verge reported the attack was stopped before being deployed in the wild.

OpenAI's answer arrived the same day. **Daybreak**, announced personally by Sam Altman, is an AI-powered cybersecurity initiative that uses the Codex Security AI agent to build threat models from an organization's source code, validate likely vulnerabilities, and automate detection of the highest-risk attack paths. The proposition is simple: let AI find the holes before another AI — or a human adversary — does.

The third piece of the puzzle came from developer **Theo** (t3.gg), who late Monday shared an alarming update from security firm Socket: the total count of compromised npm package artifacts has now reached **205 across 84 package names**, including components from UiPath and TanStack. Theo's comment: *"I hope you guys understand that this is going to keep getting worse."*

Three incidents, one day: AI built the weapon. AI stopped the weapon. And attackers quietly poisoned the supply chain that millions of developers rely on without thinking. The security paradigm is shifting faster than standards can follow.

## The Trial of the Decade Reaches Its Key Witness

San Francisco's highest-profile tech trial reached a dramatic moment Monday. The *Musk v. Altman* case — in which Elon Musk accuses OpenAI of abandoning its mission of beneficial AI development in favor of profit — called **Ilya Sutskever** to the stand. Sutskever, OpenAI's co-founder and former chief scientist, was present during the tumultuous November 2023 boardroom drama around Altman's brief ouster. He has since left to co-found Safe Superintelligence in Israel — yet he came back to defend the company. According to Wired's coverage, he testified: *"I didn't want it to be destroyed."* The statement was precisely calibrated: defending the original mission without taking any position on whether OpenAI's current direction honors it.

Simultaneously — as if the trial were happening in an adjacent room — OpenAI launched **DeployCo** (the OpenAI Deployment Company): a new corporate entity backed by **$4 billion** from 19 partners, with 150 Forward Deployed Engineers tasked with helping businesses bring frontier AI into production. Altman also posted on X that the combination of the new ChatGPT model, its personality, and its personalization has crossed a threshold for him — it *"feels like a new thing."* A qualitative shift, not an iteration.

## A New Kind of AI Takes Shape

**Mira Murati** — former OpenAI CTO and founder of Thinking Machines — published the first real details about what her company is building. The goal: "interaction models," AI systems that don't operate in isolated request-response loops but continuously absorb audio, video, and context, collaborating in real time the way humans naturally collaborate. The company blog put it directly: *"People talk, listen, watch, think, and collaborate at the same time, in real time. We've designed an AI that works with people the same way."*

The same day, **Andrej Karpathy** sketched a parallel vision on X: a progression of AI output formats from plain text → markdown → HTML → interactive visual simulations. His argument: roughly one-third of the human brain is a massively parallel processor dedicated to vision — "the ten-lane superhighway of information into the brain." AI interfaces barely exploit this yet. Audio is the preferred human *input* to AI; vision should be AI's preferred *output* back to humans. Murati and Karpathy are describing the same problem from different angles.

## The Jobs That Disappeared Quietly

**Wired** published a first-person essay Monday from screenwriter **Ruth Fowler**, who works in Hollywood. In eight months, she signed **20 contracts** across five different platforms. The work: annotating and evaluating AI training data. She's not an outlier — according to Fowler, "everyone who used to make TV" is doing the same. The term she uses is precise: *"the new waiting tables"* — AI gig work as the new fallback for people whose original careers AI largely absorbed.

**General Motors** the same day announced layoffs of hundreds of IT workers, stating plainly what it wants in their place: AI-native development, data engineering, cloud architecture, agent and model development, prompt engineering. No translation required — fluency that mattered three years ago no longer does.

Nobel Prize–winning economist **Daron Acemoglu** — whose 2024 paper questioning AI productivity claims earned him few fans in Silicon Valley — told MIT Technology Review that one of the things he watches most carefully is whether AI's labor market transformation is proceeding as equitably as tech optimism assumes, or whether the gains and losses are distributing asymmetrically. The Fowler essay and the GM press release answer his question before the journalist finished asking it.
