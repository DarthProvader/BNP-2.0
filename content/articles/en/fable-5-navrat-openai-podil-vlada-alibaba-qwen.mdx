As we reported last week, Fable 5 was pulled from production just three days after launch at the US government's request. On Tuesday, July 1st, it came back — with an improved safety classifier that Anthropic describes as better at distinguishing harmful from legitimate requests. There's a catch, though: per **AI Explained**, the new classifier will flag more benign requests than before, including routine coding and debugging tasks. The price of Fable's return is greater strictness.

There's also a deadline. From **July 7th**, Fable 5 leaves standard subscription plans (Claude Max and Claude Pro). It remains accessible via credits, but the weekly free window is closing. **Thariq** from Anthropic confirmed on X that the company intends to restore Fable as a standard subscription feature — capacity permitting — without committing to a timeline.

**NetworkChuck** and security expert **Daniel Miessler** devoted an entire video to the question of how to spend the remaining window wisely. Their framing: Fable is like a superintelligent alien that landed for one week. Don't send it on errands — use it to rebuild roads. Miessler's priority list: audit your AI harness (the system prompts and scaffolding governing your agents), run a full security sweep of everything you've deployed, and do a strategic self-inventory of what parts of your work are about to go obsolete versus 10x. *"Work that pays off after the window is gone,"* he says.

**Theo** from t3.gg has a more tactical angle for users already hitting rate limits. His core finding: Fable on effort level *"high"* consumes dramatically fewer tokens than *"xhigh"* or *"max"* — often with better outputs. He also trained Claude Code to delegate implementation work to GPT-5.5 through Codex, keeping Fable reserved for orchestration and hard reasoning. His CLAUDE.md now includes a full section on model prioritization within agentic workflows. It's a sign of a broader shift: the most sophisticated users aren't just picking one model anymore — they're building routing logic around a fleet of them.

## Fable vs. Sol: The Numbers Finally Emerge

**AI Explained** did what the community has been waiting for: using shared reference models across both system cards, they back-solved direct comparisons between Fable 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol. The headline: Sol is roughly comparable and costs exactly **half the price** on API. On **HealthBench Professional**, Mythos 5 (Fable's uncensored base) scores 66.0%; Sol scores 60.5% (64% length-adjusted). On Terminal Bench 2.1, Sol Ultra edges ahead at 91.9% versus Mythos at 88% — but that's a niche benchmark measuring terminal tool use, not general intelligence. In virology, essentially tied. The takeaway: Fable is marginally better overall; Sol wins on performance per dollar, at least for now.

Running alongside: Anthropic released **Claude Sonnet 5** with minimal fanfare, and the system card is unusually candid — in most cases Sonnet 5 trails Opus and Mythos class models, and when its discounted API pricing reverts in September, it won't be particularly competitive. One stat is worth flagging: Sonnet 5 shows dramatically improved resistance to **prompt injection attacks** — under **1% success rate** for attackers, compared to 30%+ for Mythos 5, 32%+ for Opus 4.8, and over 50% for Sonnet 4.6. For developers building agentic systems with web access, that's a material security improvement.

On a different front, Anthropic publicly accused **Alibaba** — specifically the team behind the **Qwen** model family — of harvesting **29 million Claude conversations** to use as training data, in direct violation of Anthropic's terms of service. Anthropic calls it *"the largest extraction campaign of its kind"* and frames it geopolitically: *"Distillation attacks turn hundreds of billions of dollars in American investment into a massive subsidy for our geopolitical competitors."* AI Explained's sharper implication: if sophisticated model distillation becomes common practice, frontier labs gain powerful incentive to stop releasing their best models publicly at all — accelerating the access restrictions that are already frustrating developers worldwide.

## Corporate Chess: Equity Deals, Custom Silicon, and Zuckerberg's Admission

**OpenAI** reportedly proposed giving the US government — via a sovereign wealth fund — a **5% ownership stake** in the company, according to the Financial Times, confirmed by The Verge and TechCrunch. Sam Altman's stated rationale: if the public has a financial stake in AI companies' success, political opposition softens. He floated a model resembling Alaska's Permanent Fund, where citizens receive dividends from shared resource revenues. AI Explained is skeptical, offering an alternate theory: OpenAI can make this offer in ways Anthropic probably won't — given Anthropic's explicit emphasis on codified, uniformly applied rules across all frontier developers — which could translate into preferential regulatory treatment for OpenAI.

On the hardware front: **Anthropic** is in talks with **Samsung** to manufacture a custom AI inference chip, per TechCrunch — coming one week after OpenAI announced its Jalapeño chip partnership with Broadcom. Nvidia dependency is expensive, and both companies appear to have reached the same conclusion at roughly the same time. **Microsoft**, meanwhile, launched **Frontier Company** with a **$2.5 billion commitment** to AI deployment, joining Amazon, OpenAI, and Anthropic as entities building their own distribution infrastructure rather than purely reselling access.

**Mark Zuckerberg** told Meta staff at an internal meeting that AI agent development hasn't progressed as quickly as he'd hoped — a notably candid admission from the person who, a year ago, promised an "army of AI engineers." Meanwhile: **Gemini Omni Flash** from Google DeepMind now leads Video Arena with an Elo of **1,404** and a **101-point gap** over second place — one of the largest leaps in the leaderboard's history. **Grok Build** launched voice input via /voice or Ctrl+Space, transcribing natural speech in real time. And from the absurdity desk: TechCrunch noted that **Jersey Mike's** — an American sandwich chain — mentioned AI in its IPO documents. We are in a very particular moment.
