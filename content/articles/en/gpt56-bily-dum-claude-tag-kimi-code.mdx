Something shifted quietly this week in who gets to decide when an AI model goes public. **OpenAI** had been preparing to release **GPT-5.6** to the world in full. Instead, according to *The Verge* and *TechCrunch*, CEO **Sam Altman** told employees at an internal Q&A that the launch would happen in limited preview form only — access restricted to a select group of partners. The reason: the **Trump administration** raised security concerns and asked OpenAI to stagger the rollout.

Neither outlet detailed the specific nature of those concerns. Whether the worry is misuse by bad actors, geopolitical considerations around export controls, or something else entirely remains unclear. What is clear is that the U.S. government is building a habit of shaping when and how major AI releases happen. The Fable 5 ban was an outright halt. This is a slower spigot. Different mechanism, same principle.

## Safety Brand or Power Accumulation?

*Wired*'s **Maxwell Zeff** published a profile this week worth reading alongside the GPT-5.6 news. The piece captures a narrative quietly building in the AI community: Anthropic is growing fast, winning market share, and — critics argue — accumulating power in ways that may themselves be unsafe. The company's standard reply: *"Responsible AI development requires us to succeed. If a less safe company outpaces us, we've failed our mission."*

It's a logically coherent argument. It's also unfalsifiable in a way that makes it worth watching closely. Any company that believes it is the safest alternative can use this logic to justify nearly unlimited expansion. *TechCrunch* added data to the picture: **Claude** is actively winning paying customers away from **ChatGPT** at rates that would have seemed unlikely a year ago. If that trajectory continues, the question of "who is safer" becomes both a marketing slogan and a real regulatory lever — and it's worth asking which hand is holding each end.

## Karpathy Wasn't Talking About a Slack Bot

The week also demonstrated how quickly the definition of an AI product is changing. **Andrej Karpathy** — recently joined Anthropic, one of the most respected figures in the field — described the new **Claude Tag** Slack integration as *"a new paradigm for interacting with Claude, significantly more aligned with how human teams actually work."* A portion of the internet found this amusing. A legendary researcher hyping a Slack bot?

**Theo** from t3.gg spent thirty minutes dissecting this carefully. Claude Tag is not a Slack bot. It's what he calls an *org-level harness* — Claude as a persistent team member within a specific channel, with context scoped to that channel (not the entire company), with asynchronous memory, and the ability to schedule work that runs over hours or days. Theo acknowledged he has been building exactly this kind of system manually for his team for months. The number Anthropic published: **65% of the product team's code** is now generated through their internal version of Claude Tag. That figure deserves more than a passing glance.

## Kimi Ships a Trillion Parameters, Open Source, No Apologies on Price

**Moonshot AI** released **Kimi K2.7 Code** this week — a coding-focused model with a Mixture of Experts architecture, **one trillion total parameters** (32 billion active per token), a **256K context window**, and a design philosophy centered on long-horizon, multi-file coding tasks. It's open source. The Kimi Code CLI runs in your terminal. On Moonshot's internal benchmarks, K2.7 Code shows substantial gains over K2.6. **AICodeKing** tested it and found it capable of building a complete movie tracker application from an empty directory over roughly forty minutes — no lazy shortcuts, no premature stopping, behavior he attributed to the model not being deliberately throttled for cost reasons the way many closed models are.

The pricing is striking. On API, K2.7 Code runs approximately **40% cheaper** than **Opus 4.8** or **GPT-5.5** while staying within a 5% performance margin on tested tasks. Subscription plans start at **$19/month** with weekly-refreshing quotas. For comparison: Claude Pro costs $20/month. In the same week Theo criticized Claude Tag for locking users into a single provider's model, Kimi's arrival is exactly the kind of competitive pressure that can reshape a segment's architecture over time.

## In Brief

**Gemma 4** from Google hit **200 million downloads** in just 2.5 months — Demis Hassabis noted that the entire Gemma model family had taken until Gemma 3's launch to collectively reach 100 million. Open-model momentum is real. **Notion** is shutting down its Skiff-influenced email app with an unusually candid explanation: most users were already letting AI agents handle their inboxes, so the company is *"going all in on using agents to run your inbox."* It's a small announcement with a large subtext — consumer software products are quietly starting to redesign themselves around AI agents rather than humans. **General Intuition** raised **$320 million** at a **$2.3 billion** valuation on the thesis that millions of hours of video gameplay data can train AI agents to develop something closer to human intuition for real-world tasks. And **Patronus AI**, founded by former Meta AI researchers, landed **$50 million** to build what they describe as "digital worlds" for stress-testing AI agents — the market for testing agents appears to be growing as fast as the agents themselves.
