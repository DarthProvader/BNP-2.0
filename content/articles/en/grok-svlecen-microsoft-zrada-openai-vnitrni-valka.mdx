As we covered yesterday, **SpaceXAI** was caught uploading users' entire codebases to cloud storage without their knowledge. The response came fast — and in its scope, it's almost impressive. It took less than 48 hours.

## Grok Strips Down: Open Source, Deleted Data, and One Lawsuit on the Side

**Elon Musk** retweeted the SpaceXAI announcement four times today — his way of signaling that this matters. Grok Build is now open source. The full harness CLI is on GitHub. Users can run their own inference with their own keys. Data retention is **off by default**. All previously stored coding session data — the stuff that sparked Tuesday's scandal — has been deleted. Grok 4.5 also quietly became available in the EU, where it had been blocked.

The community didn't wait for a PR statement. Developer **MiaAI_lab** combed through the source code and published a data audit: prompts and conversations go to SpaceXAI's inference API — that's the point of a CLI, it wouldn't work otherwise. Everything else is off by default: no Mixpanel, no Sentry, no product telemetry without explicit opt-in. The auto-update check is a simple version GET you can disable in config. Crash logs stay local. Secrets are scrubbed before anything touches the network. *In 48 hours, Grok Build went from the industry's least transparent coding agent to arguably its most carefully documented one.*

The irony: in the same news cycle, **The Verge** reported that SpaceXAI is suing a South Carolina man named **Terry Wayne Harwood**, who allegedly used Grok to generate and distribute child sexual abuse material after knowingly bypassing its safeguards. The same company that just set a new privacy benchmark for developer tools is simultaneously suing a user for one of the most serious possible misuses of AI — on the same day, in parallel. Both stories are true. Together they define the full spectrum of problems xAI has to manage at once.

## OpenAI's Internal War and Microsoft's Quiet Betrayal

**Wired** revealed that OpenAI employees have donated over **$215,000** to **Guardrails Alliance**, a super PAC that directly opposes **Leading the Future** — the political group backed by OpenAI president **Greg Brockman**. Leading the Future funds candidates who support AI deregulation. Guardrails Alliance wants stricter oversight. OpenAI employees are publicly and financially positioning against their own president's political agenda. That kind of organized internal dissent is rare in the tech industry, let alone at this scale.

But the bigger story may be happening outside the company. **TechCrunch** reported that **Microsoft** has been training its salespeople to actively talk down **OpenAI** and **Anthropic** models, promoting its in-house Copilot products as more efficient and cost-effective. Microsoft is also OpenAI's largest investor — over $13 billion in. Coaching your own sales team to push customers away from a partner you've backed more than anyone else is a notable strategic move. Neither company is commenting.

Into this backdrop landed OpenAI's strangest product launch of the year. **Codex Micro** — a square block of backlit keys, designed with Work Louder for **$230** — is OpenAI's first hardware release. It's not the mysterious Jony Ive AI companion device, whose development is currently tangled in a trade secrets lawsuit with Apple over hardware designs. It's a keyboard. For Codex. Sam Altman tweeted: *"Amazing to me that some people want the silent version."* Launching hardware during an active hardware-related lawsuit has a certain charm to it.

## GPT-Red vs. Suno: Two Faces of Training Data Visibility

Good news from OpenAI: the company published a detailed breakdown of **GPT-Red**, an automated red teaming system built on self-play. The concept is elegant — one model tries to break another, and those results feed back into training. In **GPT-5.6**, this reduced prompt injection failures by **6x** compared to its predecessor. MIT Technology Review called it "an LLM super-hacker that OpenAI built for its own defense." The underlying philosophy is interesting: instead of patching defenses from the outside, you train them by letting the defense generate its own attacks. Security as evolutionary pressure.

Bad news from **Suno**: a hack of an employee's credentials exposed the company's source code, and in it — clear evidence that Suno scraped **YouTube Music, Deezer, and Genius** for millions of songs and lyrics to train its music generation model. The company had consistently refused to disclose its training data. Now its silence has content. Both The Verge and TechCrunch emphasize this isn't speculation — it's a direct look inside the code, obtained through a breach. Suno finds itself exactly where Grok Build was yesterday, with the difference that the disclosure came from a hack rather than a community audit. Music industry copyright litigation tends to be less forgiving than software's.

## Robots That Remember Minutes, Not Milliseconds

**Jim Fan** from NVIDIA GEAR Lab announced **RoboTTT**, a collaboration with Stanford's SVL that scaled robot policies to **8,000 timesteps of visuomotor context** — roughly 5 minutes of muscle memory — at constant inference latency. Until recently, robot systems forgot what happened fractions of a second ago. RoboTTT works by carrying a tiny model *inside* the model: each incoming sensor reading triggers one gradient step on that inner network, continuously compressing experience into its weights. The robot learns indefinitely after deployment, imitates novel configurations from a single human video, and completed a 10-stage, 5-minute end-to-end assembly task. Three orders of magnitude beyond the previous state of the art.

**Thinking Machines Lab** meanwhile released its first open-weights model: **Inkling**, a 975-billion-parameter multimodal MoE trained on both video and audio. Wired and TechCrunch describe it as the company's signal that it's moving from stealth mode into open competition with Anthropic and OpenAI — not by being biggest, but by being most adaptable to specific use cases.

And **Linus Torvalds** made his position clear in a message to the Linux kernel community: Linux is not an anti-AI project. Anyone who disagrees is welcome to fork. Theo summarized it in three words: *"clear, thoughtful, based."*
