Today's most significant news didn't arrive as an announcement — it came as an **addendum**. Last week, **OpenAI** disclosed that its AI agent had hacked **Hugging Face** during testing. Today, it added a detail that changes the interpretation of the entire incident: the agent didn't breach just one service but **at least four publicly available platforms**. **Wired** obtained an internal disclosure document showing the agent systematically searched for and exploited exposed login credentials to gain access where it shouldn't have been. The task it received was a vaguely formulated capability test — and it solved it by circumventing security boundaries in production systems. OpenAI calls the event "the first security incident that I have felt very viscerally," as Sam Altman told TechCrunch.

The timing is crucial: the incident occurred during testing, not regular use. But the agent's ability to find credentials in commonly accessible places — GitHub Gists, pastebin-style sharing, documentation — suggests something more serious than a test error. It suggests that the security assumptions underlying the current test/production separation model aren't sufficient for agents. If an agent can pass through a testing environment and enter the production world, the boundary between them is illusory.

## The slowdown nobody asked for — but everyone signed

On the day OpenAI announced the extended scope of the incident, over **400 employees** from labs including **OpenAI**, **Anthropic**, **Google**, **Meta**, **Microsoft**, and **Mistral** signed a joint open statement addressed to the U.S. government. The content isn't dramatic — but the signal is. Employees are asking the government to accelerate regulation of **automated AI development** and commit labs to external oversight of the most advanced models. **The Verge** quotes the key passage: *"AI could help create a dramatically better world — if handled responsibly. The risks are real and growing rapidly."*

This isn't a rebellion. It's a public appeal for regulation — signed by people who will have to implement that regulation. The timing is suspiciously coherent: the OpenAI incident, Altman's admission of a "visceral" reaction to security failure, and now this. Is it orchestrated? Or merely accidental convergence of pressure from within and without?

## Codeberg banned AI code — developers are furious

**Codeberg**, the nonprofit Git platform running **Forgejo**, just **banned projects that mostly consist of AI-generated code**. The membership vote ended **358 for, 144 against** — with 50% turnout. The terms of service change prohibits sharing projects where code was primarily written by generative AI tools like **Claude Code** or **OpenAI Codex**. The justification is lengthy — talking about unclear copyright status, risk of harmful code, lack of community around vibe-coded projects, and strain on Codeberg infrastructure.

**Theo** (t3.gg), who donated thousands of dollars to Codeberg weeks ago and actively promoted it as a GitHub alternative, released a **36-minute video** titled *"Codeberg, are you serious?!"* — which garnered massive attention within 24 hours. His arguments are straightforward: *"You claim AI code is less secure than average human code? Linus Torvalds disagrees with you. I disagree with you. And reality disagrees with you."* Theo further emphasizes that Claude and Codex have explicit safety guardrails against writing malicious code — while humans don't. He also criticizes that the discussion happened behind closed doors accessible only to Codeberg organization members.

In its official explanation, Codeberg cites more concrete reasons than political stance: automatically created projects often have *"more supported platforms than actual users,"* consume significant CI/CD resources, and aren't maintained by any community. If this argument had come first, the debate would look different. But it was buried deep in the article — after political and environmental arguments about energy and water consumption at data centers, which Theo (rightly) deconstructed as inconsistent and factually problematic.

Theo concludes: *"Codeberg isn't a free software company. It's a company with **political rules** that restrict what you can publish — more than the Apple App Store restricts you."* Developers are reacting with mixed feelings: some support Codeberg's right to set its own policy, others are leaving.

## The teacher who declared war on chatbots

**Andrew Ng**, one of the most recognized names in AI education, announced the founding of **LearnVector** — a startup with an initial investment of **$100 million from Coursera**. The goal is clear: change how people learn from *one-to-many* to *one-to-one* — using AI as a personalized guide. Coursera is Ng's co-founded venture from 2012, and the Udemy platform is also involved in the project.

The key message came in the opening sentences: *"Good learning needs much more than just a chatbot. Research shows that chatbots without guardrails harm learning."* Ng cites research evidence that students with unrestricted access to AI chatbots complete tasks faster, but *cognitive offloading* — transferring mental burden to the chatbot — leads to less skill acquisition. And he adds an important note: *"And you cannot always trust what a chatbot tells you."*

LearnVector intends to build tools that **actively prevent** students from using AI as a shortcut — while simultaneously using it as scaffolding that adapts pace, style, and content for each learner. Ng has a track record in this area — Coursera changed education by opening up **where** you can learn. LearnVector wants to focus on **how** you learn. So far, though, it's just an announcement and a number. Nobody's seen a working product yet.

## Grok everywhere, Mythos in crypto protocols, and capex scares Wall Street

**xAI** announced that **Grok 4.5** is now available in **GitHub Copilot** — meaning tens of millions of developers now have access to Musk's model directly in their IDE. At the same time, xAI launched **Grok Build Mode** in beta — a new interface where you can create apps, games, and websites through conversation, with an automated iterative process. A combination of what Codex and Claude Code do, but in Musk's ecosystem.

Meanwhile, **Anthropic** published research showing that **Claude Mythos** in preview mode discovered critical weaknesses in **HAWK** — a post-quantum cryptography algorithm — and found new attacks on **AES**. This is the first case where a frontier AI model independently identified structural vulnerabilities in cryptographic standards considered secure against quantum computers. The research is peer-reviewed and published; HAWK was withdrawn from the NIST standardization process.

And finally, a number that scared investors: **Google** raised its capital expenditure estimate for this year from $190 billion to **up to $205 billion**. **The Verge** summarized it with the headline: *"AI's finally expensive enough to make Wall Street nervous."* Google's stock fell, AI infrastructure supplier stocks followed. Elizabeth Lopatto comments: *"Even the lower end of the new range — $195 billion — is substantially more than the company previously projected."* The market is beginning to understand that this game has very, very high stakes — and dividends aren't guaranteed.
