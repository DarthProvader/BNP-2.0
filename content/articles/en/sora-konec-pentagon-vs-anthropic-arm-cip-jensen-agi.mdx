## The Sora Postmortem (and ChatGPT's Amazon Problem)

It launched with enormous fanfare. It ends with a quiet announcement. **OpenAI** officially killed **Sora**, its AI video generation product, on Tuesday — and with it, a licensing deal with **Disney** collapsed. *The Verge* reports that CEO Sam Altman informed staff before the public announcement dropped. *Hollywood Reporter* confirmed that Disney ended its planned investment almost immediately upon learning of the shutdown. Sora was supposed to be OpenAI's foothold in Hollywood. Instead, it became a cautionary tale.

*TechCrunch* described the product with some relish as "the creepiest app on your phone," noting that while the underlying Sora 2 model is genuinely impressive, there was simply no sustained audience for an AI-only social video feed. The lesson here isn't that video generation failed — it's that model capability and product-market fit are two entirely different things.

In a somewhat ironic parallel, the same day OpenAI announced an expansion of its **Agentic Commerce Protocol** — a shopping infrastructure turning ChatGPT into a visual storefront with merchant integrations and side-by-side product comparisons. Yet simultaneously, the company quietly stepped back from *Instant Checkout*, the feature meant to be ChatGPT's "Amazon moment." *TechCrunch* put it plainly: "OpenAI's plans to make ChatGPT more like Amazon aren't going so well." OpenAI is running many experiments at once — not all of them are landing.

## The Pentagon Is Afraid of a Chatbot

In a federal courtroom on Tuesday, a district court judge openly questioned why the **Department of Defense** had designated **Anthropic** — the maker of Claude — a supply-chain security risk. *Wired* reported the judge described the Pentagon's move as an "attempt to cripple" the company. The case is ongoing, but the judge's skepticism toward the DoD's arguments was public and pointed.

The context matters: Anthropic is one of OpenAI's strongest competitors for government and enterprise AI contracts, and the Pentagon's designation would effectively lock it out of federal work. It reads less like a security assessment and more like a competitive maneuver — which is exactly what the judge seemed to find troubling.

Meanwhile, the other face of AI's military ambitions is on full display. The **Maven Smart System** — the Pentagon's AI-powered military operations platform built with **Palantir** and **OpenAI** — is operational and expanding. Fireship's latest video is titled "Tech bros optimized war… and it's working," and *Wired* published a book excerpt titled "Meet the Gods of AI Warfare," documenting how Project Maven's early skeptics became its most fervent believers. AI in the military isn't a future concern. It's the present.

## Arm Crosses a Line It Held for 35 Years

**Arm**, the UK-based chip design firm that spent 35 years exclusively licensing its architecture to other manufacturers, crossed a historic threshold this week: it's making its own chip. The **Arm AGI CPU** — yes, the name is intentional — is optimized for inference workloads, powering cloud-hosted AI models and agents. **Meta** is the first customer, per *The Verge*, with **OpenAI**, **Cerebras**, and **Cloudflare** also among early adopters, according to *Wired*.

This is a seismic shift in the semiconductor industry's structure. Arm was the Switzerland of chip design — neutral, ubiquitous, and comfortable staying in the background. Building its own product changes the relationship with every customer who also licenses its designs. The move comes as pressure for specialized AI hardware reaches a historic peak. For context on the capital involved: **Kleiner Perkins** closed a new **$3.5 billion fund** this week focused almost entirely on AI.

## Claude Gets More Rope — With Guardrails

**Anthropic** launched **Auto Mode** for **Claude Code**, its AI coding assistant, allowing it to execute tasks with significantly fewer approval prompts. *TechCrunch* called it giving Claude Code "more control, but keeping it on a leash." The change also extends to **Cowork** — Anthropic's collaborative AI tool — enabling autonomous computer control: opening files, browsing the web, running dev tools, all without requiring the user to be present.

One engineer tracking Claude's release cadence counted **74 product releases in 52 days**. That's not just fast. That's a pace that suggests a team in a full sprint with no intention of slowing down.

And then there's the statement of the week, perhaps the year: **Jensen Huang**, CEO of **Nvidia** — the world's most valuable company at over $4 trillion — appeared on the **Lex Fridman Podcast** and said, "I think we've achieved AGI," before walking the claim back. *The Verge* covered it with appropriate skepticism — AGI remains a deliberately fuzzy term, and Huang isn't the first tech executive to invoke it. But the fact that the CEO of the most powerful company in AI felt comfortable saying it — even momentarily — tells you something about the story this industry is telling itself right now.
