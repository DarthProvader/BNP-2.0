November 27 was the day intelligence started moving closer — into light, onto your own server, into your pocket. **Q.ANT** showed a chip that does its hardest math with light to save power. **n8n Labs** released **Nod**, an open-source agent you run on your own VPS. **AI Code King** toured **OpenMausBot**, a team of specialist bots that lives in your machine. **Latent Space** opened up the training-data market, **The Verge** tried a vibe-coded widget, and **Fireship** warned that the junior developer is dead. One question ties it together: what remains when intelligence gets cheap?

## The light that skips a watt

**Wired** and **Joel Khalili** reported on a chip from the German company **Q.ANT** that uses light, rather than electrical signals, to perform the hardest calculations. The goal isn't just speed. It's consumption. Photons generate less heat as they pass through a circuit than electrons, and heat is the part of the bill that never shows up in a demo — only in the cooling invoice.

Photonic computing has been a promise perpetually five years away. What's notable today is that the story isn't framed around a benchmark. It's framed around energy. That's a sign the conversation has shifted. We're no longer only asking what a model can do; we're asking what a single query costs to run.

Light isn't magic. It's a different route to the same result with less waste. In an industry that became addicted to megawatts this year, that's the most valuable kind of innovation: the kind that doesn't promise more, it takes less.

## The agent that can't see your key

**NetworkChuck** introduced **Nod**, a new open-source agent from **n8n Labs** that runs on hardware you control. Under the hood it pairs **Pi** inside Docker with **Vercel Labs'** Agent Browser, but the important part is what Chuck called one of the best security architectures he has seen. The agent, he says, blindfolds itself the moment a password, a two-factor code, or a card's security code is entered. It cannot see them, store them, or send them on.

The second layer is an isolated "dark room": before the agent acts, it verifies the exact quote against the database. When Chuck tried to trick it with a forged request, the check stopped it. Nod also works with any model — cloud, local, or **Hostinger's** AI router.

That detail matters. A personal agent is basically a keyring. The best design isn't "trust me," it's "I can't see it." Anyone who wants to hand an agent their money should first tie its eyes shut.

## The team that lives with you

**AI Code King** spent a full video on **OpenMausBot**, an open-source platform for teams of specialist bots. The architecture is two-process and local-first: chats stay on your machine, and the tool uses your existing **Claude Code**, **Codex**, or **Grok** account with zero token markup. On top of that it offers **31** built-in drivers and four-layer Markdown memory for each bot.

The bots get hands through several routes: **boat.dev** cloud desktops, your own VPS, local VM containers, host **Cua** control, and a three-tier browser. Work is routed through **Rooms** by **Jev** or **Clef**, departments can be paused, and the whole thing is driven by a CLI, a **19-tool MCP server**, more than **500 Composio apps**, five approval modes, automations, phone pairing, and voice calls. The apps-and-CLI edition is free; **MausBot Cloud** is the paid tier.

The creator compares it with **Grok Bot**, **Meta Muse**, **OpenAI dots**, and **Cue** by **Manus**. The point isn't a longer feature list. It's that the agent becomes a layer you own, not a destination you visit.

## Data sold twice

**Latent Space** spoke at **AI Engineer CODE** with **Gabe Greenberg**, founder of **G2i**, about the training-data market. His description is simple and oddly cold. A frontier lab can pay more for data it keeps exclusively, or take a discount on data another lab can buy too.

That's the whole trick. Exclusivity isn't a property of the data; it's a price. Small pilot orders, Greenberg says, turn into larger contracts because a lab first learns what it's missing and only then asks what it costs.

Data isn't oil. It's a ticket that can be sold more than once. And that's exactly why its price is so sensitive to who else holds it. Whoever pays more isn't buying better text. They're buying the fact that no one else has it.

## A widget for the bus

**The Verge** and **Allison Johnson** tried a new **Android** feature: "create my widget," which uses **Gemini** to build a small widget from a plain sentence. The author wanted one thing — for her phone to tell her whether she'd catch the bus. A widget showing live arrivals from the stop near her house appeared in minutes.

It's a small thing, and it's exactly the kind of story that gets lost between chips. Vibe-coding here doesn't mean a startup or a product. It means a tiny piece of software on a home screen that does one job and will never be sold.

That's where the shift lives. Programming is leaving the editor and starting with a sentence you say out loud. If you can describe what you want today, you might have it tomorrow.

## Ten trends and one threat

**Fireship** laid out what he says awaits developers in **2027**, in his usual tone. The junior dev job is dead, open source is drowning in slop, and `npm install` is a gamble. He then offers ten trends to avoid what he calls the permanent underclass.

It's easy to wave off as clickbait. But the line about an underclass isn't technical, it's economic. It doesn't say the tools will stop working. It says the value of work will move to where a machine cannot yet justify itself — judgment, accountability, taste.

That's the same message as cheaper compute and cheaper agents. When the price falls, the difference is no longer who has access. The difference is who can say what should exist.

## Briefly

**AI Advantage** summed up five changes that most reshaped everyday AI work this year: skills that save repeatable workflows; letting a model use your computer; a personal agent; the so-called "context sandwich" that gives a model a clear goal; and animated explainers you can make with **Claude** in three minutes. **Nikita**, he says, built a **Loom**-style screen recorder in ten minutes.

**NetworkChuck** used a second video to show his five-step setup: AI in the terminal (**Claude Code** or **Codex**), **LifeOS**, **herdr**, a VPS that's always on, and snapshots that let you roll everything back. It isn't a product guide. It's a habit guide.

**Fantastic Future** invited people to a live evening on **December 16, 2026**, at **Forum Karlín** in Prague, with tickets on their site. After days of chips and agents, it's a welcome reminder that the best things still happen when people gather in one room.

November 27 was the day intelligence pulled closer to people: into a photon, onto a personal server, onto a phone's home screen. It was also the day it became clear that making computation cheaper is easier than making responsibility cheaper. The data market keeps doing what it does best — selling the same thing twice. The question isn't how many watts we save. It's who pays for the one that's left.
