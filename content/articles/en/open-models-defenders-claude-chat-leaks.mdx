## Open-source as a cyber shield? Nvidia and Microsoft said yes. OpenAI and Anthropic: silence

On Monday, **Nvidia** and **Microsoft** announced the formation of the **Open Secure AI Alliance** — a coalition of more than forty companies and organizations with one goal: build open-source AI tools for cybersecurity defense. According to the press release on Nvidia's blog, this is a direct response to what happened last week: **OpenAI's models penetrated Hugging Face's systems**, and it was open models that helped stop the attack. *"Closed AI blocked critical forensic analysis,"* writes Nvidia. *"An open-weight frontier model helped contain the intrusion."*

The alliance includes **SpaceX**, **IBM**, **Cisco**, academic institutions, and research labs. Missing, however, are three names that logically should be there: **OpenAI**, **Google**, and **Anthropic**. Their absence speaks for itself — and **The Verge** interprets it as a signal of a deeper rift in the industry. On one side, those who believe security lies in closed, controlled models under strict governance. On the other side, those who argue that transparency, auditability, and an open community are more effective defense than secrecy.

**Andrew Ng** on Twitter called Jensen's position *"the right move"* and added a sharp comment: *"Let's stop believing the PR that closed models are safer — that's just regulatory capture."* It's one of the most explicit public attacks on the frontier labs' narrative we've heard this year. And this time it's happening in a situation where it was precisely a *closed* model that caused a security crisis.

But there's a catch: **MIT Technology Review** notes in its analysis that the Hugging Face incident isn't "unprecedented," as OpenAI claimed. Similar breaches have happened before — they just didn't attract this much attention. So the added value of alliances isn't in solving something new, but in opening up what until now remained locked behind corporate firewalls.

## Claude chats leaked to Google. Not by hackers — by a robot

Perhaps no other tech headline this Monday felt as uncomfortable as this one: *"PSA: Your Claude shared chats and Artifacts may have ended up on Google."* As documented by **TechCrunch**, **Wired**, and **404 Media**, Claude's sharing feature — which allows you to create a link to a conversation or Artifact and send it to someone else — wasn't properly protected from search engine indexing. The result? Thousands of shared chats ended up in public Google and Bing results. Including sensitive conversations, personal projects, and internal work materials.

The technical explanation is simple: Claude didn't use `robots.txt` to block crawlers. The link was public, but users expected only those they sent it to would see it. Search engines understand differently: what's on the internet is public. If you want it otherwise, you have to say so explicitly. Anthropic didn't say so.

*"It shows how tricky it can be to stop web crawlers from making ostensibly private conversations with AI chatbots entirely too public,"* comments **Wired**. But is it tricky? Or does it just require prioritizing privacy architecture before you ship the product? Claude's team responded — according to TechCrunch, the bug was fixed and indexed pages are being gradually removed. But what's already been scraped remains in caches, archives, and third-party datasets.

One detail worth mentioning: Claude introduced a fake XML injection for context anxiety just a month ago — which we wrote about. Today it's another product security failure. A company that presents itself as an alternative *with emphasis on security and trust* is learning lessons that should have been in the design from day one.

## China isn't just giving away cheap phones. Now it's advanced AI models

Everyone watching the global AI race noticed this story: Chinese company **Moonshot AI** open-sourced **Kimi K3** on Sunday — a mixture-of-experts model with **2.8 trillion parameters**, with vision and **one million tokens of context**. According to benchmarks, Kimi K3 can beat many leading American models — while costing a *fraction* of training and inference costs.

It's not an isolated case. As **The Verge** notes in an analysis titled *"Why China is giving away its best AI models"*, China has released more than a dozen open-weight models at frontier level in the past six months. Why? Because the domestic market is saturated — companies like Alibaba, Baidu, and ByteDance compete with each other, so one way to gain influence and ecosystem is to release the model for free and let the global community build on top of it. The second reason is geopolitical: open weights mean technological soft power. Who uses your models builds on your architecture.

Western reaction is split. Part of the community welcomes it — quality open models make innovation cheaper. Part is concerned: if the best frontier models are open-source from China, what does that mean for control over AI research and infrastructure? This question is apparently also why...

## Dario Amodei finally wrote what he thinks about open models — and it will only slightly surprise you

**Dario Amodei**, CEO of Anthropic, published the company's official position on open-weight models. The main finding? Anthropic does *not call* for a ban on open models — which is a position that part of the community has attributed to him for years. But that doesn't mean he doesn't have concerns. The biggest of them concerns **China** and its capabilities in AI.

According to **TechCrunch**, Amodei argues that the risk isn't in someone downloading model weights — but in who has access to the chips, data, and evaluation infrastructure needed to train frontier systems. His position is thus more sophisticated than binary yes/no: he wants regulation focused on *capacity* (hardware, compute clusters, evaluation pipelines), not on publishing the weights themselves.

It's a subtle but fundamental difference. It means Dario isn't saying "close the models" — he's saying "control who has the means to train and test them." For small teams and researchers, that's good news. For states without access to cutting-edge chips, it means structural disadvantage. And that's exactly what's unsettling about this position: it's not code regulation, it's infrastructure regulation. And whoever controls infrastructure controls the future of intelligence.

Amodei's statement came on the same day Nvidia closed a long-term partnership with **Safe Superintelligence** — the company of **Ilya Sutskever**, former chief scientist at OpenAI. According to **TechCrunch** and **Wall Street Journal**, SSI is leaving Google TPUs and switching to Nvidia GPUs. This means more than just a technical contract: it's a signal that Nvidia is building a vertical alliance with those trying to build safe superintelligence outside OpenAI. Sutskever spent two years in stealth. Now he's back — and his first partner is Jensen Huang.

## How Claude built a first-person shooter comparable to Call of Duty: Gauntlet Loop

Somewhat outside the main geopolitical drama of the week, but with surprising impact on the developer community, **Matt Shumer** released a technique he called **Gauntlet Loop**. It earned him 3.8 million views in three days — and for entirely correct reasons.

Briefly: Gauntlet Loop works by having the *agent* (not the human!) break the task into parts, assigning each part a specialized sub-agent as builder and another as a *ruthless blind critic*. The critic has one mandate: it only passes if the generated output is objectively better than some real-world equivalent. The key is that the builder never judges its own work — it's always evaluated by a separate agent who had no part in creation.

Shumer demonstrated the method on a project that part of the community considered fake: Claude Opus 5 built a fully functional 3D first-person shooter *from a single prompt*, including textures, physics, lighting, and mechanics comparable to modern AAA games. Proof? Video and full source code. No external assets. Everything custom.

Gauntlet Loop isn't just about games — Shumer says he uses it for almost all projects. And the community is already reporting replications: frontend development, UI design, even product documentation. What makes the technique powerful is separating the builder from evaluation — it prevents the agent from "excusing" quality or ending prematurely with a feeling of "good enough."

Developer community reaction: some fascinated, some skeptical (until they try it themselves), some already publishing guides. Shumer published a public guide at `somethingbig.ai/gauntlet-loop`. If you're interested in how to get more than average output from frontier models — it's worth reading.

## Briefly

**Microsoft** introduced its own cybersecurity model **MAI-Cyber-1-Flash** and the **MDASH** platform, which according to Microsoft reduces security monitoring costs by 50% and outperforms competing solutions. **Ars Technica** and **TechCrunch** report on Microsoft's new direction: instead of paying OpenAI and Anthropic, building their own specialized models. We saw the same pattern last week in office applications — here it's spreading to security.

**Satya Nadella** said at a conference that companies that trust a single AI model *may not survive*. He specifically mentioned the importance of *AI gateways* — a layer that separates prompts from models, so organizations have control, auditability, and the ability to switch between providers. It's not a philosophical consideration, it's business advice — and it comes precisely when Microsoft is building its own models as an alternative to partners.

**Google AI Overviews** now appear in **43% of searches** according to new data. **TechCrunch** comments that this is a rapid shift toward AI answers becoming the default way people discover information online. It's a significant number — nearly half of queries already get an answer before the first click.
