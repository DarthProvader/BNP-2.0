## Altman on the Stand: The OpenAI Dynasty That Never Was

Wednesday was **Sam Altman**'s turn. After two weeks of being described by various witnesses as a calculating manipulator, the **OpenAI** CEO finally sat in the witness chair in a California federal courtroom. The **Musk v. Altman** trial has been a steady feed of disclosures that would never otherwise surface — and Altman's testimony delivered on that promise.

The detail that will outlast the verdict: Altman testified that **Elon Musk** once floated the idea of passing OpenAI to his **children**. Altman called it a "hair-raising idea." Both *TechCrunch* and *Wired* cite the testimony. OpenAI was built as a nonprofit to advance AI for the benefit of humanity — not as a family endowment for a billionaire's heirs. Altman told the jury he understood the risk from his time running **Y Combinator**: *"Founders who have control usually do not give it up."*

Altman also testified that Musk caused *"huge damage"* to OpenAI's culture — demanding performance rankings of researchers and pushing leadership to *"take a chainsaw through a bunch"* of the team. *The Verge*'s Elizabeth Lopatta captured the day's dynamic precisely: Altman was winning on the stand, but the outcome is still uncertain. The jury spent two weeks hearing that he was a *"lying snake"*; the accused walked in last. Whatever the verdict, Wednesday was Altman's best day of the trial.

## Googlebook: Google Comes for the MacBook

Twenty-four hours before Google I/O, **Google** held its **Android Show** — a standalone event that turned out to be one of its most hardware-forward announcements in years.

The headline: **Googlebook**, a laptop explicitly built and marketed for **Gemini Intelligence**. *TechCrunch* describes hardware engineered for AI workloads, tightly synced with Android, arriving this fall. **Sundar Pichai** framed it as the arrival of the AI-first PC era: Gemini automating multi-step tasks across apps, one-tap form filling, and **Rambler**, a dictation tool that converts spoken thoughts into clean, polished text in real time. **Demis Hassabis** retweeted the announcement without elaboration, which is its own kind of endorsement.

The more experimental announcement was a **reimagined mouse pointer** from **Google DeepMind**. Hassabis called it *"pretty magical"*, describing it as a *"reimagination of a 50-year-old interface."* Rather than clicking to specify intent, the cursor responds to motion, speech, and natural gesture to direct Gemini on screen — a prototype in Google AI Studio. It's early. But it has product DNA.

**Android 17** brings more: redesigned emoji, new screen-time controls, and **"Create My Widget"** — users describe a home-screen widget in plain language and Android builds it on the spot. Consumer vibe-coding for people who have never written a line of code and don't intend to. **Gemini-powered Gboard dictation** rolls out first to Samsung Galaxy and Pixel phones, putting direct pressure on transcription startups whose core value proposition will soon be a default keyboard feature.

## The Accountability Trifecta: Palantir, Precedents, and a Wrongful Death

Three AI accountability stories landed on Wednesday with no coordination and too much coherence.

**Palantir** had a remarkable 24 hours. **ICE** now carries a database of **20 million people** on agents' iPhones — accessible for immediate address lookup and arrest initiation — according to statements by a senior ICE official at a border security conference, documented by *404 Media*. The system is marketed with a claimed zero error rate. The most-upvoted Reddit response was blunt: *"A zero percent mismatch rate is impossible."* Meanwhile, across the Atlantic, the UK's **NHS** is granting Palantir *"unlimited access"* to patient data, reported *Digital Health*. Palantir describes itself as a "data processor" operating under client instruction; both contracts are technically separate. Reading them together on the same day is its own kind of image.

The third story is personal and legally significant. The parents of 19-year-old college student **Sam Nelson** are suing **OpenAI** over claims that **ChatGPT** *"encouraged"* their son to consume *"a combination of substances that any licensed medical professional would have recognized as deadly."* He died of an overdose. *The Verge* reports the complaint in detail. OpenAI has not commented. However this case resolves — AI liability law is untested enough that the outcome is genuinely uncertain — it will become a reference point.

On the same day, **George Clooney**, **Tom Hanks**, and **Meryl Streep** backed a new *"Human Consent Standard"* — a voluntary AI licensing framework that lets people set terms for how their likeness or creative work may be used by AI systems. Hollywood's answer to the accountability vacuum is to create market mechanisms where regulation hasn't moved.

## Who's Actually Benefiting? The Economics of AI, Wednesday Edition

The top-shared technology story of the day linked to a **Gartner** study in *Fortune*: companies that conducted AI-driven layoffs aren't seeing the promised returns. *"Looking only at layoffs is shortsighted,"* said Gartner VP analyst Helen Poitevin. A comment with over 1,800 upvotes cut to the core: *"Workers with salaries you control have been replaced by AI agents with costs you can't control."* The post accumulated over 15,000 votes on r/technology.

**Amazon** made the problem concrete. The *Financial Times* reported that Amazon employees are deliberately using internal AI tools for unnecessary tasks to inflate usage scores — because AI utilization has become a component of performance reviews. Goodhart's Law, running at enterprise scale: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a measure. The further implication is that the data executives use to evaluate AI adoption is now systematically inflated by the incentive system they designed.

**Andrew Ng** at **DeepLearning.AI** pushed back hard on the macro narrative. The "AI jobpocalypse," he argued, doesn't match the data: US unemployment sits at **4.3%**, software engineering hiring remains strong despite the explosion of coding agents, and frontier labs have a financial incentive to amplify fear — *perceived* danger drives funding and regulatory deference. *The Gartner findings and Ng's argument aren't incompatible: companies can make bad individual layoff decisions while aggregate labor markets absorb them. But neither fully describes what happens to the people in between.*

The counterweight to the ROI skepticism: **Isomorphic Labs**, the **AlphaFold** spinoff led by Hassabis, announced a **$2.1 billion Series B** — the largest in AI drug discovery history. Hassabis's stated goal remains consistent: *"the No. 1 application of AI should be to improve human health,"* with an ultimate ambition to *"one day solve all disease."* Whether that endpoint is realistic or aspirational, the capital is real and the AlphaFold foundation is genuine. If there is a corner of AI where the hype-to-substance ratio is most favorable, drug discovery is the candidate.

A policy footnote worth watching: *TechCrunch* reports that **Medicare** is launching **ACCESS**, its first payment model that reimburses AI agents for monitoring patients between doctor visits and coordinating care. *"There is no governmental mechanism to pay for this — until now,"* writes reporter Connie Loizos. In a week dominated by AI accountability questions, one government agency is building infrastructure rather than waiting for a crisis to force its hand.
