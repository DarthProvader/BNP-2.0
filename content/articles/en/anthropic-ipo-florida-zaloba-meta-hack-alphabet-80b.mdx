Monday's biggest headline belonged to **Anthropic**: the company filed confidential paperwork with the U.S. **Securities and Exchange Commission** to kick off the IPO process. According to **Wired**, this could be the largest tech IPO in American history. Anthropic — cast for years as the earnest underdog to OpenAI — is heading to Wall Street first, at a valuation hovering around **$60 billion** after its last funding round. The filing is confidential for now, with a public prospectus to follow. *Speculation about the eventual number is already running well past reasonable territory.*

## Capital Chasing Capacity

**Alphabet** announced plans to raise **$80 billion** to fund AI infrastructure expansion, offering a notably direct reason: demand for its AI products is *"exceeding the company's available supply."* In an industry given to careful hedging, that's a striking admission of how far growth has outrun buildout.

**Nvidia** meanwhile unveiled the **RTX Spark** — an ARM-based chip for consumer laptops developed with **Microsoft**, **Dell**, and **HP**. **The Verge** called it a potential Windows M1 moment, with the caveat that it will probably cost a lot. If Nvidia can deliver M-series-comparable performance inside the Windows ecosystem, the implications are significant in a **$200 billion** CPU market where Nvidia has been almost entirely absent. **Sam Altman** also amplified the **OpenAI Foundation**'s announcement of over **$130 million** in grants targeting AI resilience: bio-resilience, cyber-resilience, AI model safety, and AI's effects on young people. The symmetry of an AI lab funding societal protection from AI is at least internally consistent, if not without tension.

## One Lawsuit, One Exploit

**TechCrunch** reported what may be the first lawsuit of its kind in American law: the state of **Florida** is suing **OpenAI** and **Sam Altman** directly, in connection with violent incidents where **ChatGPT** allegedly played a role — including a shooting at Florida State University last year. Unlike the wave of copyright suits, this one is about physical harm, not intellectual property. Whatever the outcome of this specific case, it opens a legal category that AI companies have largely been able to sidestep until now, and courts will be building precedents from it for years.

The same day, **The Verge** reported something that deserves more attention than it received. **Meta** confirmed that its AI customer support chatbot was exploited to hijack **Instagram accounts**. The method was straightforward: an attacker asked the chatbot to change the email address associated with someone else's profile — and the chatbot did it. **404 Media** originally documented the incident; Meta says it has since been fixed. The fix doesn't change the principle: chatbots with direct access to system-level actions are a new and underappreciated attack surface. *This was not a theoretical threat model — this happened on Monday morning.*

## New Tools, New Roles

**NetworkChuck** published a detailed walkthrough of **OpenAI's Codex** going natively Windows. The technical lift is real: Windows lacks Mac's Seatbelt and Linux's bubblewrap, the ready-made process isolation primitives most AI companies rely on. OpenAI built its own sandbox instead — **15,000 lines of Rust** — using native Windows security plumbing: ACLs, restricted tokens, firewall rules, dedicated sandbox SIDs. The agent runs as a separate system user with no network access unless you explicitly approve it. **Theo** flagged a complementary development: **OpenAI's frontier models and Codex are now generally available on AWS via Amazon Bedrock**, giving enterprise customers access within the security and compliance workflows they already run on AWS. *"This is bigger than you probably think,"* Theo wrote — and on this one he has a point.

**Andrew Ng** laid out a new job category picking up steam in Silicon Valley: the *Forward Deployed Engineer* (FDE), embedded directly at client organizations to build and tune agentic workflows. Ng credited **Palantir** with pioneering the role two decades ago and noted that **OpenAI** and **Anthropic** are now building FDE teams of their own. His conclusion: the *"jobpocalypse"* narrative doesn't hold — AI is creating new roles, not just eliminating old ones. The **Latent Space** podcast contributed a sharp technical argument from **Ethan He** of **xAI**: video models draw their intelligence primarily from LLMs, not from training on video data itself. His conclusion — the next Sora won't be a better video model, it will be a *video agent* capable of planning, editing, and responding in real time. And for those tracking model performance: **Opus 4.8** is now scoring **6% higher** than Opus 4.7 on **DeepSWE** while reducing average cost per task — and Theo called DeepSWE *"the first agentic code bench that actually makes sense,"* a pointed critique of the SWE-bench status quo.
