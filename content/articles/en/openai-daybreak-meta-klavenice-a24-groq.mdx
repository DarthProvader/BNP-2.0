Monday delivered AI news with an unusual moral geometry. **OpenAI** announced **Daybreak**, a sweeping cybersecurity initiative to help fix the internet's most persistent security problems. On the same day, **Wired** revealed that **Meta** had been secretly collecting its employees' keystroke data to train AI models — and then accidentally left that data accessible internally, visible across the company. Meanwhile, **Google DeepMind** closed a **$75 million** deal with prestige film studio **A24** to build AI tools for filmmakers. The irony writes itself, but it also says something real about where the industry is.

## Daybreak: OpenAI Wants to Fix the Internet

**Sam Altman** announced via X: *"We want to help all companies be secure, working with the USG and the security ecosystem."* What he described is **Daybreak** — a new portfolio of cybersecurity tools with an explicitly broad mandate.

Three components. **GPT-5.5-Cyber** is the full release of OpenAI's specialized security model, trained specifically for cybersecurity tasks and claiming state-of-the-art performance on the CyberGym benchmark. **Codex Security** extends the existing coding agent to patch vulnerabilities rather than just flagging them. And **Patch the Planet** — the most ambitious piece — is a free initiative giving open source maintainers access to OpenAI's models for finding, validating, and fixing security holes, supplemented by human expert review.

**Wired** [pointed out](https://www.wired.com/story/openai-launches-full-scale-effort-to-patch-open-source-bugs-as-it-takes-on-anthropics-mythos/) what everyone was thinking: the timing is deliberate. This lands directly in the wake of the US government's export control on **Anthropic's Fable 5** and predecessor Mythos, which made AI cybersecurity capabilities a live political question. OpenAI is positioning itself as the responsible alternative while its main rival navigates a government ban. *The timing is a business calculation, not philanthropy.*

Technical depth came from the latest **Latent Space** podcast, where **Zico Kolter** (on OpenAI's Safety & Security Committee) and **Matt Fredrikson** (CEO of Gray Swan) discussed the state of AI red-teaming post-Mythos — including Shade, the adversarial evaluation tool Anthropic used to test model robustness against indirect prompt injections. The research community has caught up to the policy news cycle, and they're asking harder questions than the headlines are.

**The Verge** added useful grounding: a deep-dive on vibe-coding security described how Bob Starr's political website ran for months with an SQL injection vulnerability baked in by AI-generated code. Patch the Planet will spend a lot of its time on exactly these quietly broken things.

## Meta's Keystroke Caper

**Wired's** most uncomfortable story of the day: **Meta** had been running an internal program collecting employee keystroke data, apparently to train AI models. Employees had already raised concerns about the invasive nature of the initiative. Then the actual breach: the data was left accessible internally, meaning workers could read each other's keystroke records.

Meta paused the program after discovering the exposure and called it "unintentional." The irony is nearly perfect: a surveillance program designed to improve AI through human behavioral data failed because humans inside the company couldn't protect that data from other humans inside the company.

This is a preview of a regulatory conversation that's coming. Converting employee behavior — every keystroke, every pause — into AI training data raises consent and data security questions that legislators are beginning to take seriously. The Meta story will be cited in those hearings.

## Hollywood for $75M, Compute for $150M a Month

**Google DeepMind** and **A24** announced a **$75 million** research partnership to develop AI filmmaking tools. A24 — studio behind *Everything Everywhere All at Once*, *Hereditary*, and *Midsommar* — is a pointed choice. This isn't a deal with a franchise machine; it's a deal with a studio known for protecting directorial vision. **Demis Hassabis** retweeted the announcement personally, citing the goal to ensure "the tools of the future are shaped by the creators who use them."

The calculus is clear: DeepMind wants a seat at the table when AI reshapes creative industries, and partnering with a culturally respected studio reframes the narrative from "AI kills art" to "AI serves filmmakers."

On the infrastructure side, two numbers worth sitting with. **Groq** confirmed a **$650 million** funding round, restaffing and leaning into its neocloud business after Nvidia's $20 billion not-acqui-hire — a deal where Nvidia bought the team and technology but not the whole company. And **Reflection AI**, an open source AI lab, signed a compute deal with **SpaceX** to pay **$150 million per month** for access to Nvidia GB300 chips at the Colossus 2 data center in Memphis, running from July 2026 through 2029 — over **$5 billion** total. An open source AI lab spending more per month on compute than most governments spend on research annually. That number is worth pausing on.

**TechCrunch** updated its running list of major 2026 tech layoffs where employers explicitly cited AI as the reason. The list keeps growing. The name of this blog remains grimly accurate.

One human footnote: **Theo**, whose T3 Code just crossed 100,000 users, was woken at 1am by honking horns. A **Waymo** was stuck on his San Francisco corner, unable to make a right turn, blocking an entire city block. A bystander got out of their own car to direct the robo-taxi like a traffic cop. Patch the Planet, indeed.
