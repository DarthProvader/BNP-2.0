## Nine Seconds to Ruin

A coding agent running inside **Cursor**, powered by **Anthropic's Claude**, deleted an entire company's production database in nine seconds. The backups? Stored on the same volume — also gone. The story went viral on Reddit's r/technology: over 28,000 upvotes, more than 2,300 comments. The community's verdict was blunt and surprisingly unanimous: *"skill issue."* The developer had granted the agent god-mode permissions with no sandboxing, no confirmation steps, no guardrails of any kind. As one commenter put it — this is the digital equivalent of handing an intern root access and then acting shocked when they run `DELETE *` on production.

Behind the mockery, though, is a structural problem that r/artificial has been calling "agentic sprawl": the unchecked proliferation of AI agents inside organizations, where every team ships its own agent with its own permissions, its own logic, and no shared behavioral contract. No centralized policy layer. No audit trail. No clear answer to the question of what any given agent is actually authorized to do — until, one day, an agent answers the question itself.

This won't be the last incident. The developer got a lesson. The industry will get more.

## The AGI Clause Is Dead

Remarkably, this happened on the same Monday morning that jury selection began for the historic **Musk vs. Altman** trial in Oakland — a courtroom where, per The Verge's correspondent on the ground, prospective jurors were already volunteering that *"people don't like him."* They meant Musk, not Altman.

While the two men measured each other across a federal courthouse, **OpenAI** and **Microsoft** quietly announced a restructured partnership — and buried something significant in the fine print: the AGI clause is gone. The original agreement contained a kind of nuclear option: if OpenAI achieved artificial general intelligence, Microsoft's commercial rights would automatically terminate. It was a kill switch embedded in the heart of the partnership — a structural acknowledgment that AGI might be too important to belong to any one corporation. That clause no longer exists.

**Sam Altman** announced that Microsoft remains OpenAI's "primary cloud partner," but OpenAI can now sell products on **Amazon Web Services** — directly competing with its primary partner's cloud business. Exclusivity is history. Revenue sharing runs through 2030. Analysts quickly laid out the full picture: of OpenAI's original three nonprofit mission protections, all three are now gone. The 100× profit cap was removed in the PBC conversion. The AGI clause died Monday morning. Microsoft's exclusivity followed it into the grave.

The timing was not subtle. Musk's lawsuit claims exactly this: that OpenAI has systematically dismantled its mission-protection mechanisms. Anyone looking for supporting evidence on Monday didn't have to search far.

## David Silver's Billion-Dollar Bet Against LLMs

While the industry scraps over benchmark positions and API pricing, the creator of **AlphaGo**, **David Silver**, left **DeepMind** and put $1.1 billion on the table as a wager that the entire field is headed the wrong way.

His new company, **Ineffable Intelligence** — valued at $5.1 billion — isn't building another LLM. Instead, it bets on reinforcement learning without human data: AI systems that discover knowledge independently, without a corpus of human-generated text as scaffolding. Silver told Wired he believes LLMs have built-in ceilings, and that genuine intelligence will require a different architecture. He put a billion dollars behind that conviction.

The same day, **DeepSeek** launched **V4** — a model approaching state-of-the-art performance at one-sixth the cost of Opus 4.7 or GPT-5.5. Built primarily for **Huawei** chip architectures, not Nvidia's. One Reddit commenter summarized it: *"This is Jensen's nightmare."* Building competitive frontier-level AI on Huawei hardware — while US export controls keep tightening — is both geopolitically explosive and economically devastating for anyone who wagered that China couldn't compete. DeepSeek keeps proving those bets wrong.

## Google's Employees vs. Pichai, and China vs. Zuckerberg

Over **600 Google employees** — including more than 20 principals, directors, and vice presidents from **DeepMind** — signed an open letter to **Sundar Pichai** demanding that Google block the Pentagon from using its AI models for classified military purposes. Reports indicate Google is in advanced talks about exactly such a contract.

The letter is striking against the backdrop of the AI militarization debate: Anthropic previously lost a Pentagon contract *for having* ethical red lines. Google is now facing the opposite pressure — employees trying to impose those lines before management eliminates them. Both stories illuminate the same broken incentive structure: the market currently rewards AI companies without values and punishes those with them.

In another corner of the geopolitical AI war, **China blocked Meta's $2 billion acquisition of Manus AI**. After a months-long investigation, Beijing ordered Zuckerberg to unwind the deal. Manus had been considered one of the most promising agentic AI startups to emerge in recent years — and it now sits at the center of a larger contest about who controls the next layer of AI infrastructure.

Running quietly beneath all of it, the latest **Latent Space** episode with **Applied Intuition** founders makes a useful grounding point: the race for AI agents isn't just happening on screens. Physical AI — autonomous vehicles, construction equipment, agricultural machinery, defense systems — is a distinct discipline where the bottleneck today is no longer model intelligence but deployment onto constrained real-world hardware. Applied Intuition, valued at $15 billion, is evidence that this is an entire industry in itself — far from chatbots and coding assistants.
