This morning, **OpenAI** opened the gates. **GPT-5.6 Sol**, **Terra**, and **Luna** are now publicly available — and the internet immediately resumed a debate it had been having in NDA-protected whispers for months: is Sol or **Fable 5** actually better? Today, the embargo broke.

## Rottweiler vs. Owl: The Community Weighs In

The most-quoted take of the day came from **Mitchell Hashimoto**, HashiCorp co-founder, who had tested both models for roughly a month. His framing was instantly memorable: Sol is *"a charismatic, efficient, talented coworker you're jealous of."* Fable is *"a genius recluse that is brilliant at its fixations but doesn't go out, doesn't date, and you don't want to hang out with them much."* He landed on Sol as his daily driver, keeping Fable for highly targeted debugging sessions with clear reward functions.

**Sam Altman** retweeted it with *"tbh i dont think sol gets that many dates either"* — a rare moment of CEO-level self-deprecation that the internet appreciated. **Peter Gostev** reached for different animals: Fable is a *"wise owl, very thoughtful and well-spoken,"* Sol is a *"rottweiler who grabs the problem by the throat and won't let go."* Gostev added a data point worth sitting with: when he tasked Fable with building a benchmark, the model returned a polished-sounding result in 40 minutes — but it was *"vibe-based slop."* Sol worked for six hours and shipped tested, working code.

**Matt Shumer**, who had early access to Sol, was direct: *"For almost every task I tested, Fable was quite a bit better, and more agentic to boot — one Fable turn does what many Sol turns do."* He still called Sol an amazing model and predicted OpenAI *"will have an answer to Fable soon."* **Ethan Mollick** offered the calibrated consensus: *"Both Sol and Fable represent jumps over previous models and have opened a large gap with the next-best AIs. If you're doing work where better intelligence matters, those two models are your only choices."*

Alongside Sol, **OpenAI** also launched **GPT-Live-1** — a new voice model that *The Verge* diplomatically described as *"better at shutting up."* The new architecture can speak and listen simultaneously, handles conversational pauses more naturally, and is designed with live translation as a primary use case. After months of teasing an upgraded voice mode, this is the concrete product.

## Grok 4.5 and the Benchmark That Wobbled

While Sol commanded attention, **xAI** — Elon Musk's SpaceX AI division — quietly dropped **Grok 4.5**. Musk called it an *"Opus-class model, but faster, more token-efficient and lower cost."* **TechCrunch** applied appropriate skepticism: "Opus-class" is a marketing label without a precise specification, and current comparisons suggest the positioning is closer to where Anthropic and OpenAI were last autumn. But **Theo** from t3.gg wrote that Grok 4.5 is *"good enough that I think I have to do a video on it"* — and from a developer who had given Grok little attention, that's a meaningful signal. Theo had earlier predicted an xAI comeback and posted simply: *"Called it."*

The more diplomatically interesting move came from **OpenAI's blog**: an analysis titled *"Separating signal from noise in coding evaluations,"* questioning the reliability of **SWE-Bench Pro**. The timing is striking. Fable 5 leads SWE-Bench Pro at 80.3%; Sol trails. OpenAI published a paper arguing the benchmark is unreliable on the same day it launched the model currently losing on that benchmark.

Benchmark problems are real — **AI Code King** noted in a video this week that **GLM 5.2**, a fraction of Fable's price, beats GPT-5.5 on the same leaderboard (62.1 vs. 58.6%). A metric that doesn't account for cost tiers has structural issues. But publishing the critique on launch day conflates science with PR in ways that don't flatter either.

## HalluSquatting, Microsoft's Defection, and Beijing's Move

**Ars Technica** surfaced a concerning security story: researchers found that hackers can use nine of the most popular AI tools to assemble massive botnets through a technique called *"HalluSquatting."* The mechanism is elegantly malicious: an LLM hallucinates a nonexistent software package name. An attacker registers that package. AI-generated code then automatically installs malware without anyone issuing an explicitly malicious command. The attack exploits the property most central to current LLMs and hardest to patch: the inability to say *"I don't know."*

On the business side, **Bloomberg** and **Silicon Angle** confirmed that **Microsoft** is replacing **OpenAI** and **Anthropic** models with its own in-house AI in some Office applications, primarily to cut costs. The irony is thick: Microsoft is one of OpenAI's largest investors, and Anthropic is a key commercial partner. Yet the company is systematically building model independence. When the biggest enterprise customer decides to self-supply, the rest of the market watches carefully.

From geopolitics: **Reuters** reports that **Beijing** is weighing restrictions on overseas access to Chinese AI models, including **DeepSeek**. If implemented, the global open-source AI community would lose one of its most important sources of high-performance models. The logic mirrors Washington's chip restriction playbook: strategic assets shouldn't flow freely.

## Where the Capital Is Going

**TechCrunch** reports that **Lovable** — which lets users build web applications in natural language — is in talks to raise **$300 million** at a **$13.2 billion valuation**, double its previous mark, in a round expected to be led by **Menlo Ventures**. Among "vibe-coding" platforms, Lovable stands out for having its own agentic harness rather than a wrapper UI — in a market where one model update can obsolete an entire product category, that's a meaningful distinction. The same week, **SambaNova** raised **$1 billion** at an **$11 billion valuation** for AI chips, and **Prime Intellect** closed a **$130 million Series A** for enterprise AI agents. Money is moving from the user interface layer down through the application stack to the silicon. Every level of the infrastructure is now a fundable thesis.
