[As we reported yesterday](/articles/en/fable-5-ban-siri-ai-apple-wwdc-musk-trillionar), Friday evening delivered something unprecedented: a US government export control directive that forced **Anthropic** to take its two most capable models — **Fable 5** and **Mythos 5** — offline for every user worldwide. Today, the picture of how it happened has become considerably clearer. And considerably more complicated.

## Friendly Fire: How Amazon Helped Extinguish a Competitor's Model

The central figure in the story now has a name. According to **The Verge** and **TechCrunch**, it was **Andy Jassy** — Amazon's CEO — who personally conveyed concerns about Fable 5's security risks to senior officials in the Trump administration this week. Amazon had produced its own cybersecurity research paper demonstrating the seriousness of a discovered vulnerability: through a sequence of steps, researchers were able to break through Fable 5's guardrails and surface Mythos-level capabilities — including identifying exploitable vulnerabilities in source code in ways the government deemed unacceptable.

That paper served as the basis for the export control directive. **Amazon** is, of course, one of Anthropic's largest investors, with billions of dollars committed to the company. It also has deep access to internal information — and runs its own competing AI models through **Amazon Bedrock**. *A conflict of interest that the developer community has been quick to notice.*

A detailed thread from **David Sacks** on X adds another layer. According to Sacks, the jailbreak was brought forward by a highly credible partner trusted by both Anthropic and the government, and the report went directly to **Dario Amodei**. The White House reportedly gave Dario a clear choice: patch the vulnerability, or take the models down. Dario refused both options, arguing that the capabilities exposed were already available in other frontier models — including OpenAI's GPT-5.5. The government issued its directive; Anthropic complied in the most radical way available, pulling the models for everyone.

Theo's YouTube short, one of the first public accounts of the situation, captured the mood with characteristic restraint: *"I'm tired of living in unprecedented times."* His tweet marked simply *"RIP"* referenced his earlier sardonic question — whether **Andrej Karpathy** had joined Anthropic specifically to access Mythos for ML research without restrictions. Karpathy, a Slovak-Canadian national, is now, as a foreign-national Anthropic employee, locked out of both models under the directive.

India is using the entire episode as an argument for AI sovereignty. TechCrunch reports that Indian tech leaders are calling for investment in domestic models, arguing that depending on American providers that can be regulatorily suspended overnight is a dangerously fragile strategy.

## Beijing Says No: Meta Loses Its $2 Billion Bet

A parallel geopolitical squeeze arrives from the opposite direction. **Meta** has begun unwinding its acquisition of **Manus** — the Chinese AI agent startup that built a reputation in early 2025 as arguably the world's most capable autonomous agent. Meta paid $2 billion for its stake. **Beijing** has now ordered the deal reversed, TechCrunch reports.

The outcome is stark: Meta spent $2 billion, the deal collapses, the technology gain evaporates. Viewed alongside Washington's Friday shutdown of Fable, the scene is revealing — both superpowers are rewriting the rules of the AI game in real time, and the companies are caught in the middle.

## The Bill Arrives: Google, OpenAI, and KPMG Face Accountability

While geopolitics was reshaping the AI map, three legal and regulatory stories arrived that would each have owned their own headlines on any quieter day.

**Wired** reports a landmark court ruling: **Google** has been found legally liable for false statements generated by its **AI Overviews** feature. The core principle of the ruling: a company that designs, trains, operates, and manages an AI system must assume legal responsibility for damages caused by its outputs. The argument that *"the model made it up, not us"* does not constitute a defense. This ruling could fundamentally alter how tech giants deploy generative AI in products used by hundreds of millions of people daily.

**OpenAI** faces its own storm: TechCrunch reports that state attorneys general from multiple US states have opened an investigation into the company. The scope is unusually broad — from advertising policies to the handling of health data. The specific states have not been disclosed.

And then there is the story that would be funny if it weren't so instructive. Consulting giant **KPMG** was forced to retract its own report on enterprise AI adoption because the report itself contained hallucinations. An AI system wrote an analysis about AI and simply invented some of the data. TechCrunch's verdict: *"AI once again proved to be an unreliable source of information about AI."*

## After Fable: GLM-5.2 Arrives at the Right Moment

Quietly, amid all the chaos, an interesting alternative has appeared on the market. **Z.AI** has released **GLM-5.2** — a successor to GLM-5.1 featuring a **one-million-token context window**, planned open-weight release, and MIT licensing. YouTuber **AI Code King**, given early access, benchmarked the model at 81.43 on his custom suite — roughly six percent below Opus 4.8 and Fable. The **GLM Coding Plan** starts at $8 per month, while Codex or Claude runs approximately ten times that.

The model works with any coding tool — Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode. The timing is difficult to overstate: precisely as Anthropic takes its best models offline, a Chinese open-source alternative arrives at a fraction of the cost with nearly comparable capabilities.
