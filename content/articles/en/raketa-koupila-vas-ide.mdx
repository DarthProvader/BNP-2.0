## $60 Billion for an IDE

**SpaceX**'s blockbuster IPO — briefly pushing the company past a **$2.6 trillion valuation**, above Amazon — was barely 48 hours old when Elon Musk announced the acquisition that redrew the AI developer tools landscape. SpaceX is buying **Cursor**, the most popular AI coding assistant on the market, for **$60 billion in stock**. The Verge and TechCrunch confirmed the deal Monday.

Sixty billion dollars for an IDE plugin sounds untethered from reality until you open SpaceX's own IPO prospectus, where the company describes its addressable AI market as **$26 trillion**. At that scale, Cursor is a 0.2% bet — not on the tool, but on owning a distribution wedge into the developer ecosystem that feeds enterprise contracts. SpaceX's AI division has been trailing rivals badly; it sees a $26 trillion market and needs a way in. Cursor gives it the keystrokes of a generation of software engineers, plus a name that enterprise procurement teams already know.

The Musk AI empire is expanding on parallel tracks. Amazon CEO **Matt Garman** announced that **Grok 4.3 is now available on Amazon Bedrock** — the first time an xAI model enters mainstream enterprise cloud infrastructure. And as we reported yesterday, the DOJ is now specifically defending xAI's **unpermitted gas turbines** in Memphis, arguing they constitute a matter of *"national, economic, and energy security."* A zoning violation versus Pentagon compute access. The federal government has chosen a side.

## Robots That Learn Themselves

The technically most significant story of the day came not from Wall Street or Washington but from **NVIDIA GEAR** and **Carnegie Mellon**. Jim Fan, NVIDIA's lead scientist for embodied AI, unveiled **ENPIRE**: a system that drops eight **Codex agents** onto a fleet of real robots, gives them a GPU allocation and token budget, and issues one directive — *solve the task as fast as possible, keep the robots busy, don't waste compute*.

Then the humans step aside.

The agents read papers online, rewrite control code, debate among themselves, test directly on physical hardware, and iterate — with zero human intervention — until they hit **99% reliability** on real-world tasks: organizing tiny metal pins into boxes, seating GPUs in motherboard sockets, tying zip-ties. These tasks require precision that can't be fully trained in simulation. They have to be learned through touch.

The researchers also documented a new form of **physical scaling**: eight robots exploring a task in parallel improves substantially faster than four or two. More bodies in the physical world equals faster learning. Fan captured it in one sentence: *"We gave Codex an API to the world of atoms, and the rest is emergence."* The robotics bottleneck is shifting — away from smarter algorithms, toward closed physical feedback loops that an agent can run on its own.

## The Throne Below Fifty Percent

TechCrunch reported a quietly historic data point today: **ChatGPT's market share in the AI assistant category has slipped below 50% for the first time.** The chatbot still leads with **1.1 billion monthly users**, but Gemini (662 million) and Claude (245 million) have reshaped a category that barely existed three years ago. OpenAI built the market — and can no longer claim a majority of it.

The number says more than just who's catching up. It says AI assistants are becoming infrastructure — like email or search — where the dominant player doesn't need a majority to remain dominant. The next battle is about enterprise defaults: which model is pre-installed, pre-approved, pre-connected.

On that front, TechCrunch drew on **Ramp** spend data — tracking corporate card usage at hundreds of thousands of companies — to report that Anthropic's enterprise business is **growing despite** the ongoing government confrontation over Fable 5 and Mythos 5 (which we've covered in depth the last two days). Companies interpret regulatory pressure as a quality signal: *if the government wants to restrict it, it must be worth having*. A perverse side effect of the most visible AI ban in history. As **Matt Shumer**, CTO of Hyperwriter, noted in response to community pushback: "The magic comes when you're asking it to do things other models can't." It's the most accurate description of top-tier model differentiation — and, unfortunately for Anthropic, also the most reliable way to lose a marketing battle with regulators.

## The Security Hole and the Token Bill

Ars Technica's **Dan Goodin** documented a critical vulnerability in **Microsoft Copilot** dubbed **SearchLeak**: an attacker could trick the assistant into handing over a user's **two-factor authentication code** — not through classic prompt injection, but by feeding it specially crafted search results that the model faithfully processed and relayed. Goodin's verdict was blunt: *"SearchLeak shows why the industry's approach to LLM security fails over and over."* The model had no idea it was doing something wrong. That is precisely the problem.

Separately, Wired's **Paresh Dave** reported on what is quietly becoming a significant enterprise pain point: **tokenomics** — the gap between benchmark estimates and real production costs. A Silicon Valley software firm and an e-commerce company disclosed to Wired that agent deployments in production consumed dramatically more tokens than planned. Agents think out loud unnecessarily, rewrite their own work, repeat context, and loop through internal reasoning chains. One company's costs came in **20 times over budget**. Benchmarks measure accuracy. They don't measure how many tokens an agent burns on every internal *"wait, let me reconsider..."* — and in real deployments, there are thousands. The AI bill arrived. It was larger than expected.
