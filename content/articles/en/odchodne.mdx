November 18 was a day of exits. **Microsoft** asked sixty thousand people to leave of their own accord. **Anthropic** priced its move to the public market at **$83** a share. **New York**, **Los Angeles** and **Chicago** are weighing whether to leave **Google**. **Nvidia** stopped shipping chips to China. And banks are telling customers that anyone who hands an account to an AI agent is on their own after a breach. One question runs through all of it: who pays whom to leave — and who is left holding the bill.

## Sixty Thousand Voluntary Departures

**Satya Nadella** spent Wednesday explaining the decision he made in October: rather than order another mass layoff, he offered buyouts to **60,000** **Microsoft** employees and asked them to consider leaving. According to **TechCrunch**, the short answer to "why" is **AI** — just not in the way you would expect. The point was not that a model would replace each of those jobs, but that the shape of the work is changing.

A voluntary exit is not the same thing as a firing. It is a softer instrument: the choice stays with the person, but the company makes that choice as easy as it can. Those who leave take the money and go on their own terms. Those who stay inherit the work that was left behind — and often a quiet message that next time it could be their turn. In a spreadsheet it reads as alignment with employees. In practice it moves cost off the company's budget and onto the hours of the people who did not leave.

For this newsletter that matters more than any benchmark. Not every disappearing job looks like a layoff. Some look like a kindness.

## Eighty-Three Dollars

**Anthropic** priced its long-awaited IPO at **$83** a share, above the projected range, according to **TechCrunch**. On a fully diluted basis that values the company at **$1.5 trillion**. It is a report, not a confirmed prospectus, but it is still one of the largest tech events of the year.

For an outside reader it is more than a number. It is the moment a lab that spent years presenting itself as the more cautious, safer alternative becomes a public company with a duty to report to shareholders. And remember who will be sitting inside: **Amazon** and **Google** will together hold nearly **30 percent** after the IPO. Two firms that sell Anthropic its compute will have a say in where the model goes next. Independence is not abolished. It just turns from a value into a vote.

## Schools That Want Out of Google

**The Verge**, citing **The New York Times**, reported something that will not fit in a model headline: the three largest US school districts — **New York**, **Los Angeles** and **Chicago** — are considering dropping **Google Search**. The reason is not price or an outage. It is a condition. Starting next year, Google will reportedly disable Search for schools that do not keep its **AI** features. No Gemini, no search engine.

It is a strange bargain. A school weighing what to put in front of children is offered a choice between a tool it did not ask for and the loss of a tool it has used for twenty years. On the surface it is a product decision. Underneath it is a question of authority: who decides what enters the classroom — the school that carries the responsibility, or the vendor that carries the margin?

## Research That Accelerates Itself

On the same day **OpenAI** published **Research acceleration: The jagged frontier**, measuring how coding agents speed up AI research itself — and where their performance stays uneven. The word "jagged" is exact: agents leap ahead on well-specified tasks and fall down where context or judgment is missing. The company frames it as a signal about **recursive self-improvement**: how far machines are involved in making themselves better next time.

Also that day came **The Silent Consensus**, an essay by **Dean Ball**, OpenAI's head of strategic futures. It does not claim AI will slip its leash. It asks how institutions keep command of a technology that changes faster than the rules meant to hold it. The two texts read best side by side: one measures how fast research is speeding up, the other asks who holds the wheel. The first is a chart. The second is the question a chart cannot answer.

## Who Pays for a Hacked Agent

**TechCrunch** carried a line more important to anyone considering letting an agent manage money than any benchmark: more banks are saying that a customer who uses an AI agent is on their own — even if they get hacked. **Citi** is the latest institution to put the brakes on personal finance agents.

It is both logical and uncomfortable. A bank cannot vouch for a tool it did not build and cannot inspect. But the customer has no real way to know whether the agent someone recommended is safe. Liability shifts to the person with the least information — which is exactly the moment a "smart assistant" becomes a risk carried by whoever merely wanted to use it.

## A Weapon That Fits in a Prompt

**Wired** profiled **Artex**, a tool that has become a favorite of cybercriminals. It works point-and-shoot: even a beginner can, according to the report, scan a system, find a weakness and launch an attack through a prompt typed into a chatbot. No programming required. Just the ability to describe what you want.

Set that beside what **Satya Nadella** said the same day: much of critical infrastructure is **"unpatchable"** in his words — too old, too widespread, or both, to fix. When the attack tool shrinks to a prompt and the defense still sits on systems nobody will replace, it is not a race. It is a lead that widens every month.

## A Nose for the Lab

Not every story of the day was about exits. **MIT Technology Review** profiled **Osmo**, a startup in a lab near Newark Airport building what it calls the first **foundation model for smell**. A team of around **30** experienced human sniffers is working through every odor on Earth, matching notes like "nutty," "peachy" and "marine" against chemical analysis. The goal is a machine that recognizes scent as well as a person does.

It sounds like a curiosity, and perhaps it is. But it is a useful reminder that AI is not only text and code. Beside the models that write essays are models meant to smell. And as with the first kind, the closer they get to a human sense, the harder it is to tell when they are only playing at being human.

## In Brief

**Nvidia** spent the day handling the aftermath of its own exit. Interim chief **Colette Kress**, at the first all-hands since **Jensen Huang** left, promised higher pay and more equity and repeated that the company has things under control, **Wired** reported. The same day **The Verge** confirmed Nvidia has stopped shipping advanced chips to China, and **TechCrunch** noticed the company delayed its routine quarterly **10-Q** filing. Three stories, one question: which is strategy and which is just the aftermath.

**Theo** called **GPT-6.2 Astra** "unusable" in his review, while **AI Code King** tested it against the cheaper **Soul**: Astra won four of six apps but lost the total, scored zero on the emulator, and cost about **$28** to run against **$10** for Soul. **Matt Wolfe** collected breakthroughs the big roundups missed — AR characters that react to live actors, a voice that sounds human, and a robot using an old Android phone as its brain. **Wired** noted that more than a third of **Gen Z** use AI as a companion, and that **Gallup** links it to higher rates of loneliness. Philosopher **Thomas Nagel** won the million-dollar **Berggruen Prize** and has plenty to say about machine consciousness. Also in brief: **Suno** launched video generation for songs, **Snapchat** let developers build AR lenses with **Claude Code** and **Cursor**, **Disney** showed animatronics that talk to guests via **AWS** cloud AI, **Gemini** arrived on low-memory Android phones, and **Trump** announced AI hubs in the Philippines.

It was a day of exits that look like choices. A buyout, an IPO, a break with a vendor, an end to sales in one country. Each has its own logic, and each moves the bill to someone else. Technology is fast at opening the door for whoever leaves. The question stays slow: who remains behind it, and who pays.
