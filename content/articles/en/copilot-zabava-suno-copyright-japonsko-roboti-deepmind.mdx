## The Fine Print Nobody Read (Until Now)

It didn't take a leak or a whistleblower. Just someone reading the terms of service. **TechCrunch** reporter Anthony Ha flagged what might be the most revealing sentence in enterprise software this year: **Microsoft** has included language in **Copilot's** terms of use explicitly describing the product as being *for entertainment purposes only*, warning users not to place undue reliance on its outputs.

*Let that sit for a moment.* Microsoft simultaneously sells Copilot to enterprises as a productivity revolution — legal drafting, financial analysis, strategic decision support — while its lawyers write "entertainment purposes" into the fine print. Both cannot be true. Either Copilot is reliable enough for material business decisions, or it's a toy. The legal department chose the latter. The sales department never got the memo.

The deeper irony is that none of this actually changes how people use AI. On r/artificial this week, a thread went viral about someone who refused to follow the manufacturer's hair dye instructions because ChatGPT had told them differently. The original instructions were in their hands. They chose the AI anyway. As user IsThisStillAIIs2 put it: "once someone successfully defers to AI a few times, it becomes uncomfortable to override it even when better information is right in front of them." Microsoft can call it entertainment all they want. Users have already promoted it to oracle.

**The Verge** also examined **Grammarly's** strange transformation this week — from a grammar checker into a full AI writing assistant that's earned the nickname "sloppelganger": producing prose so smoothly generic it's hard to tell whether a person or a model wrote it. Again, the accountability for output quality? Conveniently blurred.

## Japan: Where Robots Save Rather Than Replace

While the West debates whether AI is taking jobs, TechCrunch reporter Kate Park filed a dispatch from Japan that cuts a very different angle. Japan — facing a structural labor shortage driven by an aging population and historically low birth rates — is moving physical AI systems from pilot programs into full-scale real-world deployment.

Autonomous warehouse carts. Robotic arms in manufacturing. Navigation and care-assistance systems in elder-care facilities. Sectors where willing human workers simply don't exist in sufficient numbers have become the proving ground for what Park calls "physical AI." Her headline said it best: *"In Japan, the robot isn't coming for your job; it's filling the one nobody wants."*

This distinction matters more than the current discourse acknowledges. The "AI takes jobs" debate is relevant in economies with labor surplus and structural unemployment. In Japan's context, physical AI is a genuine economic lifeline — a way to sustain care and production capacity that would otherwise collapse. Different economies, different problems, different AI. It's a shame this nuanced framing gets flattened into the same monolithic fear.

On the research frontier, **Google DeepMind** published a study letting an LLM rewrite its own game theory algorithms — and the results outperformed expert-designed solutions. User Budget_Coach9124 on r/singularity captured the mood well: "we keep being surprised that these things can optimize in domains they weren't specifically designed for and it just keeps happening." The more pointed question came from user EightRice: "If AI can now rewrite the rules of the game, who constrains the mechanism designer?"

## Suno's Copyright Shrug

Following [yesterday's story](https://berounampraci.cz/rakety-v-oblace-claude-za-mrizi) about folk musician Murphy Campbell discovering AI-generated fakes of her music being distributed under her name, The Verge's Terrence O'Brien focused this week on the platform side of AI music's legal nightmare: **Suno**.

Suno formally prohibits users from uploading copyrighted material and promises its detection systems will catch violations. In practice, the system fails. Users report generating covers and derivative tracks that clearly draw from protected songs with minimal friction. Suno is already one of the defendants in the music industry's broad copyright lawsuit against AI music platforms. Every incident The Verge documents adds another data point for plaintiffs.

The pattern is exhaustingly familiar: launch product, market it as creative revolution, then when legal questions arrive, point at the detection system and say it isn't perfect. That isn't a response to copyright infringement. It's a shrug. And copyright law was built for reasons that didn't evaporate when generative AI arrived.

## Two Small Signals Worth Tracking

**Gemini** embedded in **Google Maps** earned a rare positive hands-on review this week. The Verge's Allison Johnson admitted going in skeptical — and came out impressed. The Maps AI handled full-day itinerary planning, blending cultural suggestions with practical logistics, all via natural language. *"You know what? It's kind of great."* After months of Google taking AI criticism, it's a noteworthy moment of genuine utility cutting through the noise.

And quietly, leaker **@levelsio** posted screenshots on X of what appears to be **GPT-Image-2** — OpenAI's next image generation model, reportedly tested under a pseudonym alias. The screenshots suggest significantly stronger world knowledge and cleaner text rendering than its predecessor. OpenAI has not officially acknowledged the model's existence. But running it quietly under an alias is itself a kind of statement.
